A muscle, level by level

A skeletal muscle is built in levels. The whole muscle is made of bundles of fibres, the fascicles. Each fibre is a single cell, very long and with many nuclei, packed with myofibrils. Each myofibril is a chain of sarcomeres, and each sarcomere is an array of actin and myosin filaments. The Microscope shows you the first few levels.

Skeletal muscle
  1. Open a section of human skeletal muscle at low power . Some of its fibres were cut lengthways and some across. You can see how they’re grouped into fascicles, with pale connective tissue between them.
  2. Choose the Label tool and click on the connective tissue between two fascicles. Call the label Perimysium. It carries blood vessels and nerves into the muscle. Like the endomysium round each fibre and the epimysium round the whole muscle, it runs into the tendon, so it helps to pass the pull of the fibres on to the bone.
  3. Now go to some fibres cut across, at 10× . Each of these shapes is one fibre, and so one cell.
  4. With the Label tool, label one of the nuclei at the edge of a fibre, and call it Nucleus. Most of the nuclei you can see belong to the fibres, though a few between them belong to the cells of capillaries and connective tissue.
  5. Measure three fibres. Choose the Measure tool and drag across a fibre at its narrowest, from one edge to the other. Then do the same for two more fibres. If one goes wrong, delete it in the Measurements panel and measure it again.
Why the narrowest width?

A fibre that was cut at a slant looks longer than it should in one direction, but its narrowest width hardly changes. That’s why pathologists who measure the fibres in a muscle biopsy use the narrowest width too. They call it the lesser diameter.

In a thin slice like this, each fibre shows only a few nuclei, but along its whole length a fibre has hundreds or thousands. Why does it need so many? And why might they sit at its edge?
A fibre is a huge cell. The fibres on this slide are about 60 to 80 µm across, and a fibre can be many centimetres long, sometimes running the whole length of its muscle. One nucleus couldn’t make enough mRNA for the proteins of so much cytoplasm, so each nucleus looks after the stretch of fibre around it. The nuclei come from the many myoblasts that fused to make the fibre as it developed. Satellite cells, which lie just outside the fibre under its basement membrane, can fuse with it to add more nuclei when it grows or is repaired. Keeping the nuclei at the edge leaves the middle of the fibre to the myofibrils. A row of nuclei down the middle of a fibre is usually a sign that it has regenerated after damage, as happens again and again in Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

Smaller than the scan can show

A sarcomere is only about 2.5 µm long, so it spans just five pixels of this scan, and the pale I band in each is only a pixel or two wide. That’s at the very limit of what the scan can show, so don’t expect to see the cross-striations. Look at some fibres cut lengthways at 40× , though, and you may just make out faint lines running along them, where the myofibrils, each about a micrometre across, lie side by side.

As a sarcomere shortens, which of these stays the same length?

Why T-tubules?

In the second lecture you saw that the action potential spreads from the surface of a fibre into its middle along the T-tubules, so that the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases its Ca²⁺ right next to every myofibril. Why not just let Ca²⁺ in through the surface membrane? Your measurements can tell you. The time a molecule takes to diffuse a distance x is roughly t = x² / 2D, where D is its diffusion coefficient. For Ca²⁺ in water, D is about 800 µm² per second. Each show me button types a line into RStudio’s Console for you. Press Enter to run it.

In R
  1. Read your measurements into R: m <- read.csv("~/microscope/measurements.csv")
  2. Pick out the widths of your fibres, in µm: w <- subset(m, slide == "skeletal-muscle" & kind == "length")$value . Then work out their mean: mean(w)
  3. Halve it, to get the distance from the edge of a fibre to its middle: x <- mean(w) / 2 . Then work out how long Ca²⁺ would take to diffuse that far, in seconds: x^2 / (2 * 800)
  4. Inside the fibre, Ca²⁺ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum has only about 1 µm to go to reach the filaments. Work that out too: 1^2 / (2 * 800)
How long would Ca²⁺ take to diffuse from the surface of one of your fibres to its middle, and how long from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to the filaments? What does that tell you?
For these fibres, 60 to 80 µm across, the distance from the surface to the middle is about 33 µm, and 33² / 1600 gives about 0.7 s. From the sarcoplasmic reticulum to the filaments, 1 µm, it’s about 0.6 ms. That’s a thousand times quicker for a distance only 33 times shorter, because the time goes with the square of the distance. A twitch develops its force within a few tens of milliseconds of the action potential, so Ca²⁺ coming in through the surface would be far too slow. The edge of the fibre would contract long before the middle, and much of the Ca²⁺ would be pumped away before it got there. In real cytoplasm it would be slower still, because proteins bind Ca²⁺ as it goes. The T-tubules solve the problem by carrying the action potential into the middle of the fibre within about a millisecond, so that the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases its Ca²⁺ right next to every myofibril, and the whole width of the fibre contracts at once.

If you have time

Smooth and cardiac muscle
  1. Open the smooth muscle of the oesophagus at 10× , the same magnification as the skeletal muscle. Its cells are long, thin spindles, each with one nucleus in the middle, packed together in bundles. Unlike skeletal and cardiac muscle, it has no striations, though this scan is too coarse to show you the difference.
  2. A smooth muscle cell is only about 5 µm across at its widest. How long would Ca²⁺ take to reach its middle from the membrane? 2.5^2 / (2 * 800)
  3. Now open the heart, at 40× . Cardiac muscle is striated, like skeletal muscle, but its cells are much smaller, with one or two nuclei in the middle. They branch, and they’re joined end to end by intercalated discs, the dark lines that cross the fibres every 100 µm or so. Don’t mistake them for striations, which are dozens of times closer together, and too fine to see in this scan.
Smooth muscle has actin and myosin too. Why does it have no striations, and why doesn’t it need T-tubules?
Its actin and myosin aren’t lined up in sarcomeres. Bundles of filaments run at an angle across the cell. They’re anchored to dense bodies in the cytoplasm, which do the job of Z discs, and to adhesion plaques on the membrane, and intermediate filaments link the dense bodies to each other. With nothing in register, there’s nothing to make stripes. Because the bundles pull at many angles, the whole cell bunches up as it contracts, and it can shorten much further than a skeletal muscle fibre. It’s so thin that Ca²⁺ coming in through its membrane has at most 2.5 µm to go, which takes about 4 ms (2.5² / 1600 = 0.0039 s), so it doesn’t need T-tubules. It has small pits in its membrane, caveolae, instead, and it contracts far more slowly than skeletal muscle anyway.

Force, velocity and power

The heavier the load, the more slowly a muscle can lift it. A. V. Hill described the relationship in 1938 with the equation (F + a)(v + b) = (F0 + a)b, where F0 is the isometric force, the force the muscle exerts when it’s held so that it can’t shorten, and a and b are constants. If you measure the force as a fraction of F0 and the velocity as a fraction of the maximum, vmax, and take a to be a quarter of F0, which is typical, it becomes v = 0.25(1 − F) / (F + 0.25). Each show me button types a line into RStudio’s Console for you. Press Enter to run it.

In R
  1. Make Hill’s equation into a function: hill <- function(f) 0.25 * (1 - f) / (f + 0.25) . Then make a series of forces from 0 to 1, and work out the velocity at each: f <- seq(0, 1, by = 0.01) and v <- hill(f)
  2. Plot the force–velocity curve: plot(f, v, type = "l", xlab = "Force (fraction of the isometric force)", ylab = "Velocity (fraction of the maximum)")
  3. Power is force × velocity. Work it out, and plot it: p <- f * v and plot(f, p, type = "l", xlab = "Force (fraction of the isometric force)", ylab = "Power")
  4. Find the force at which the power is greatest: f[which.max(p)]
  5. Compare a light load with a heavy one, 0.05 of F0 and 0.5 of it: hill(0.05) / hill(0.5)
Why does a muscle shorten more slowly as the load gets heavier?
Because the load is carried by the cross-bridges that are attached at that moment. With no load, the filaments slide as fast as the cross-bridges can cycle, which sets vmax. That depends on how fast the muscle’s myosin uses ATP, and it’s higher in fast fibres. The heavier the load, the more cross-bridges have to be attached at any one time to bear it, so each spends more of its time attached and pulling, and less of it detaching and moving on, and the filaments slide more slowly. At the isometric force, F0, all the attached cross-bridges are needed just to hold the load, and the muscle doesn’t shorten at all.
At what force is the power greatest, as a fraction of the isometric force?
At 0.31 of F0, where the velocity is also 0.31 of vmax, so the power there is only about a tenth of F0 × vmax. Power is zero at both ends of the curve. With no load the muscle shortens fastest but exerts no force, and at F0 it exerts its full isometric force but doesn’t move. A muscle gives its most power at intermediate loads and speeds, about a third of its maximum force and velocity.
Say a muscle can just hold 100 g, so that 5 g is 0.05 of its isometric force and 50 g is 0.5 of it. How many times longer does it take to lift the 50 g mass the same distance?
About 4.75 times: hill(0.05) / hill(0.5) is 0.792 / 0.167. The time it takes to lift a mass through a given distance is the distance divided by the velocity (leaving out the moment it takes to get going), so ten times the load takes almost five times as long. That’s the example that the first short-answer question in chapter 4 starts from.

Your arm is a lever

A muscle can only pull, and it can only shorten by a fraction of its length. The skeleton turns that pull into movement. Bones act as levers, the joints are their pivots, and where a muscle’s tendon is attached decides how much force it gives up for speed and range.

The biceps, in Anatomy and R
  1. (biceps brachii, in the front of the upper arm).
  2. Once you’ve found it, the panel on the right tells you about it. Press Fade the others there, then Frame it, and follow the biceps down past the elbow. Its tendon attaches to the radius, just below the elbow.
  3. The tendon pulls on the radius about 4 cm from the elbow, and your hand is about 35 cm from it. Work out how many times as far, and as fast, the hand moves as the biceps shortens: 35 / 4 . Then work out the force, in newtons, that the biceps needs to hold 5 kg in your hand with your forearm horizontal: 5 * 9.81 * 35 / 4
What does your arm gain from having the biceps attached so close to the elbow, and what does it lose?
It gains speed and range. Every centimetre the biceps shortens moves the hand 8.75 cm in the same time, so the hand moves 8.75 times as far and as fast as the muscle shortens. It loses force. To hold 5 kg (49 N) in the hand with the forearm horizontal, the biceps would have to pull with 8.75 times as much, about 430 N, if it worked alone (the brachialis and brachioradialis share the load), and more once you add the weight of the forearm itself. Most of the levers of the limbs work like this, trading force for the speed and range of movement that limbs need. Different jobs are geared differently, though. Digging animals such as armadillos have a long olecranon, the lever the triceps pulls on, which gives their forelimbs force, while running animals such as horses have muscles that pull close to the joints, at the top of long, light legs, which gives them speed.
How do a bicycle’s gears let you cycle at close to your maximum power, whatever the slope of the road?
Your leg muscles give their most power at one particular combination of force and speed, about a third of their maximum of each, which for cycling means a particular rate of pedalling. The gears change how fast your legs have to turn for a given speed of the bicycle, and so how much force they have to exert. Uphill, a low gear lets your legs keep turning quickly against a force they can manage, though the bicycle moves slowly. On the flat, or downhill, a high gear stops your legs having to spin faster than they can usefully go. Either way the muscles stay near the load and speed at which they give their most power. The levers of the skeleton are gears too, but fixed ones, which is one reason why muscles that do different jobs have different fibre types and shapes.

In most people the soleus has more type I fibres than the gastrocnemius. How does that fit what each muscle does?
The soleus is a postural muscle. It works almost all the time you’re standing, to stop you falling forwards over your ankles, so it needs fibres that hold a force economically and don’t tire. Those are type I fibres: slow and oxidative, with plenty of mitochondria, capillaries and myoglobin. Most of the soleus’s fibres are type I, often 70 to 90 per cent. The gastrocnemius is used for quick, powerful movements, such as jumping, sprinting and pushing off, and about half its fibres are fast, type II fibres, which contract quickly but tire sooner, especially the glycolytic type IIb (called IIx in people). The nervous system recruits the small, slow motor units first, for standing and walking, and adds the large, fast ones only when more force is needed. That’s the size principle.

“How are movements on the cellular and molecular scale used to produce movement of whole animals?”

Thinking about the title

The title covers an enormous range of scales. In one cross-bridge cycle a myosin head moves an actin filament 5 to 10 nm, and a running animal covers metres in a second. The question is how you get from one to the other, so a good essay is about the steps in between: how the tiny movements of molecules are added up, switched on together, controlled, and passed on to the skeleton. These questions might help you plan.

  • The motor. What does one myosin head do in one cross-bridge cycle, and where does the energy come from?
  • Adding up. What happens to the shortening when thousands of sarcomeres are joined end to end, and to the force when myofibrils and fibres lie side by side?
  • Switching on. How does an action potential at the surface of a fibre make every sarcomere in it contract at once? Chapter 1 has some numbers for this.
  • Control. How does the nervous system grade the force of a whole muscle?
  • From muscle to limb. How do tendons and the levers of the skeleton turn the shortening of a muscle into the movement of a limb, and what do they trade? Chapter 2 has some numbers for this too.
  • Whole animals. Which examples will you use? And do all animals move in the same way?

Don’t try to write down everything you know about each step. Choose the points that show how one scale produces the next, and say how as you go.

There’s no single right answer here, so bring yours to the supervision. Many essays on this title spend most of their words on the cross-bridge cycle and excitation–contraction coupling, and then rush the end: how the force of many fibres is graded and passed through tendons and levers to move a limb, and what that means for a whole animal as it walks, runs, swims or flies.
