Optional: gradients, resting potentials and the action potential

≈ 15 min

An optional exercise. Work out equilibrium potentials and a resting potential in R, model the membrane as a circuit, and set off an action potential in a simulated neuron.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It’s good preparation for the short-answer questions, though, and you’ll need R for the practical question anyway.

Equilibrium potentials, in R

In a frog muscle fibre, the K+ concentration is about 140 mM inside and 2.25 mM outside, and the Na+ concentration about 10 mM inside and 120 mM outside. At 20 °C the Nernst equation becomes E = (58 / z) log10([ion]out / [ion]in), in mV, and for K+ and Na+ z is +1. Each show me button types a line into RStudio’s Console for you. Press Enter to run it.

Work them out
  1. Work out EK: 58 * log10(2.25 / 140)
  2. And ENa: 58 * log10(120 / 10)
  3. The resting membrane is permeable to both ions, though much more to K+. The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) equation weights each one by its permeability, so make it a function of the ratio PNa / PK: ghk <- function(r) 58 * log10((2.25 + r * 120) / (140 + r * 10)) . Then try a few ratios: ghk(c(0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1))
What did you get for EK and ENa, and what happens to the resting potential as the ratio PNa / PK goes up?
EK is about −104 mV and ENa about +63 mV. With a ratio of 0.001 the GHK equation gives −102.7 mV, close to EK, and as the ratio goes up the potential moves towards ENa: −93.3 mV at 0.01, −71.4 mV at 0.05 and −57.7 mV at 0.1. The resting potential lies between the two equilibrium potentials, and nearer the one for the ion the membrane is more permeable to.
A frog muscle fibre rests at about −90 mV. Roughly what is the ratio of PK to PNa?

The membrane as a circuit

In the parallel-conductance model of a membrane, each kind of ion channel is a conductance (drawn as a resistor, since conductance is 1 / resistance) in series with a battery at that ion’s equilibrium potential. The membrane potential settles where the currents through them cancel out, at the average of the equilibrium potentials weighted by their conductances.

Run the model
  1. Open the model and run it . The voltmeter reads the membrane potential. It settles at about −70 mV, between E(K+) at −90 mV and E(Na+) at +60 mV, but much nearer E(K+), because the K+ conductance is far larger.
  2. Click the switch labelled Na+ switch to close it. Many more Na+ channels open, and the membrane potential swings towards E(Na+). Read it off, then click the switch again to open it.
With the switch closed, the membrane potential rises to about +47 mV, but not all the way to E(Na+) at +60 mV. Why not?
Because the K+ channels are still open. The membrane potential is the average of the two equilibrium potentials weighted by their conductances, and with the switch closed that’s (10 × −90 + 101.5 × 60) / 111.5 ≈ +46.5 mV, with the conductances in nanosiemens. It would only reach E(Na+) if Na+ were the only ion the membrane let through. The same is true at the peak of a real action potential.

An action potential

Stimulate a neuron
  1. Open the Neuron simulation and press Stimulate Neuron. Watch the channels open and close, and the ions move through them, as the action potential passes.
  2. Tick Potential Chart under Show, and stimulate the neuron again to see the action potential on the chart. As soon as it has fired, try to stimulate it once more. For a while, the Stimulate Neuron button won’t work.
In the simulation the neuron rests at about −65 mV, and it uses Hodgkin and Huxley’s values for the squid axon: E(Na+) about +50 mV and E(K+) about −77 mV. Does the membrane potential reach either of them during the action potential? Why not?
No. It peaks at roughly +30 to +40 mV, below E(Na+), and after the spike it dips to about −73 mV, close to E(K+) but not quite there. By the peak, the Na+ channels are already inactivating and the voltage-gated K+ channels are opening, so the membrane is never permeable to Na+ alone. During the after-hyperpolarization the K+ permeability is higher than at rest, which pulls the potential towards E(K+), but some permeability to other ions remains, so it doesn’t quite get there. It’s the same reason the circuit didn’t reach E(Na+).
Why won’t the simulation let you stimulate the neuron again straight away?

Optional: where does nicotine act?

≈ 15 min

An optional exercise. Use acetylcholine, nicotine and a ganglion blocker on a piece of guinea-pig ileum in the Organ bath to work out where nicotine acts.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the last two lectures, on synapses and the autonomic nervous system.

The guinea-pig ileum is a short length of small intestine. Its smooth muscle has plexuses of nerve cells in its wall, the enteric ganglia, and parasympathetic nerves act on it through them. Postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors on the muscle, which make it contract. The ganglia themselves are cholinergic too, but their receptors are nicotinic, and a different type from those at the neuromuscular junction. Drugs that act at different points along that path let you work out where something acts. In the Organ bath, a piece of ileum hangs in warm salt solution, and its contractions are drawn on a chart.

Acetylcholine and nicotine

Two agonists
  1. Set up a fresh piece of ileum , and make the clock run 20 times faster . This button gives everyone the same piece of ileum, so your numbers should be close to the ones in the model answers.
  2. Add acetylcholine to give 10−7 M in the bath . Once the contraction has reached its plateau , wash it out . The response goes into the Results under the chart.
  3. Now add nicotine to give 10−5 M . Again, take it to its plateau and wash it out.
Nicotine makes the ileum contract too. Which receptors do you think it acts on?

Blocking the ganglia

Add hexamethonium
  1. Put hexamethonium (10−4 M) in the solution used for washing, and wash the bath with it . Let it equilibrate for 10 minutes . That also gives the nicotinic receptors time to recover, as nicotine desensitizes them.
  2. Add nicotine (10−5 M) again, take it to its plateau, and wash it out.
  3. Then add acetylcholine (10−7 M) again, take it to its plateau, and wash it out.
Compare the responses in hexamethonium with the ones before. (In the Results table, each response is also given as a percentage of the largest contraction so far.) What do they tell you?
In this tissue, hexamethonium cut the response to nicotine from about 97% to about 11% of the largest contraction, but left the response to acetylcholine unchanged, at about 100%. So nicotine acts at the ganglia. It excites the enteric neurons through their nicotinic receptors, and they release acetylcholine onto the muscle. Acetylcholine added to the bath acts on the muscle’s muscarinic receptors directly, so blocking the ganglia doesn’t affect it. Hexamethonium blocks the nicotinic receptors of autonomic ganglia but hardly touches those of the neuromuscular junction, which is one sign that the two are different types.

If you have time

Wash the hexamethonium out and let the tissue recover for 10 minutes . Then put atropine (10−8 M) in the washing solution , wait another 10 minutes, and try acetylcholine and nicotine again.

Atropine blocks the responses to both acetylcholine and nicotine. Why both?
Because both end in the same place. Acetylcholine acts on the muscle’s muscarinic receptors directly, and nicotine acts through the ganglia, whose neurons release acetylcholine onto those same muscarinic receptors. Atropine is a competitive antagonist at muscarinic receptors, so it blocks the last step of both. (In this tissue, atropine at 10−8 M cut both responses to about 13% of the maximum.) Atropine comes from deadly nightshade, Atropa belladonna. It dilates the pupils, which is how belladonna got its name.

The practical question: the thyroid gland

≈ 40 min

Back to last week’s hormones. You’ll photograph and label a thyroid follicle in the Microscope, measure the area of its lumen and work it out in R, and then describe how T3 and T4 are made.

The practical question

(a) Use the digital microscope to examine a histological section of the thyroid gland. Choose an appropriate magnification, and use the labelling tool to label a thyroid follicle, its lumen and a thyroid follicular cell, and take a photograph that shows your labels. Measure the area of the lumen of your follicle, and use R to report it.

(b) In your own words, and using your labelled photograph to support your narrative account, describe the steps involved in the synthesis of the T3 and T4 hormones. Write no more than 200 words.

You’ll come back to T3 and T4 later in the course, in more detail, so this is good revision. You may need to look up the structure of the thyroid gland to answer (a), and you can, but write your answers in your own words.

What you’re looking for

The thyroid gland is made of follicles. Each is a hollow ball, lined by a single layer of follicular cells, and its lumen is filled with colloid, which is mostly stored thyroglobulin and stains pink. Between the follicles there are capillaries and thin strands of connective tissue. You’ll see follicles of many different sizes, cut at different levels, so choose one that has been cut through near its middle.

Photograph a follicle

In the Microscope
  1. Open the thyroid gland and look at it at low power first. Almost all of it is follicles, each a ring of cells around pink colloid.
  2. Find a follicle you can see the whole of, with an unbroken ring of cells round it. This one works well . At 20× the whole follicle fits in the view, and you can still pick out the single follicular cells and their nuclei. For a bigger follicle, 10× may be better.
  3. Choose the Label tool and click on the edge of your follicle. Call the label Thyroid follicle.
  4. Add a second label in the middle of the colloid, and call it Lumen.
  5. Add a third on one of the cells in the wall of the follicle, on its nucleus, and call it Follicular cell.
  6. Now measure the lumen. Choose the Area tool , click your way round the inner edge of the follicular cells, and click the first point again to finish. In many follicles the colloid has shrunk away from the cells and left a pale gap. That happened when the tissue was processed, so measure to the cells, not to the edge of the colloid.
  7. Take a photograph with the camera . Take it last, so that it shows your three labels, the outline you measured and the scale bar. It’s saved in your files, in ~/microscope/pictures.
Putting things right

To move or rename a label, click on it again with the Label tool, where you can change its text or delete it. A measurement you don’t want can be deleted in the Measurements panel. If you take the photograph again, the new one is saved alongside the old one.

The area of the lumen, in R

Every measurement you make in the Microscope goes into the file ~/microscope/measurements.csv, one row each, and R can read it. Use R to pick out your lumen and report its area.

In R
  1. Read the file into a table called m: m <- read.csv("~/microscope/measurements.csv")
  2. Pick out the areas you measured on the thyroid slide: lumen <- subset(m, slide == "thyroid-gland" & kind == "area") . Then look at them: lumen[, c("label", "value", "unit", "objective")]
  3. Keep the last one, which is your lumen, in µm²: area <- tail(lumen$value, 1) . Then work it out in mm², and as the diameter of a circle with the same area, in µm: area / 1e6 and 2 * sqrt(area / pi)
What is the area of your follicle’s lumen, in µm² and in mm²?
It depends on the follicle you chose. The one that show me one opens has a lumen of about 13,000 µm² (0.013 mm²), the same area as a circle about 130 µm across. The follicles on this slide range from about 50 µm to over 300 µm across, so anything from a few thousand µm² to well over 50,000 µm² is reasonable, as long as it matches your photograph.

Answer the question

In Supervision work
  1. Open the paper , read its first page, and press Start.
  2. Part (a) is the first question. Press Insert picture in the toolbar and choose your photograph from your files (it’s the newest one). Under it, give the area of the lumen, and the magnification you chose, with a sentence on why you chose it.
  3. Then write part (b), on how T3 and T4 are made, in no more than 200 words. Use your photograph as you go, to show where each step happens: in the follicular cells, or in the colloid of the lumen.
Writing part (b)

A narrative account tells the story in order, from start to finish, so make sure each step leads on to the next. The word count under the box turns red if you go over 200 words. Nothing stops you typing, so you can write a draft first and then cut it down.

How short answers are marked

Each of these questions is marked out of five, with roughly a mark for each correct, relevant point. So answer in bullet points, and make at least five, each a separate point. You don’t need an introduction or a conclusion. A point earns its mark when it’s specific: it names the thing, says what it does, and gives a number or an example where one helps.

A vague point, and a better one

Say the question were What does the sodium pump do?

  • Too vague: “It keeps the gradients going.”
  • Worth a mark: “It pumps three Na+ ions out of the cell and two K+ ions in for each ATP it uses, against their electrochemical gradients.”

The two questions

  1. How can POSITIVELY charged potassium ions generate a NEGATIVE value of EK?

They’re the last two questions of the paper, after the practical question. Try them as you would in the exam, without your notes, and give each about 10 minutes. To start a bulleted list, press the bulleted list button in the toolbar, and then Enter for each new point.
