Cardiac muscle

Last week you looked at skeletal muscle. Cardiac muscle is striated too, but its cells are built and joined to each other differently, and those differences are much of what makes the heart work as a pump.

Cardiac muscle, in the Microscope
  1. Open a section of the wall of the left ventricle, at 20× . These cells were cut lengthways. Each usually has one nucleus, in the middle of the cell, and the cells branch and join their neighbours.
  2. Look for short, dark lines that cross the cells, often in steps. These are the intercalated discs, where one cell meets the next. They’re thicker and darker than any cross-striations you can make out, and much further apart. Choose the Label tool and label one of them Intercalated disc. Zoom in if that helps.
  3. Label a nucleus too, and call it Nucleus. The brown granules at the ends of some of the nuclei are lipofuscin, a pigment that builds up in heart cells with age.
How are cardiac muscle cells joined to each other, and why does that matter for the heart as a pump? Compare them with skeletal muscle fibres.
At the intercalated discs, the cells are joined by two main kinds of junction. Desmosomes and other anchoring junctions hold the cells together and pass the force of each cell on to the next, so that the pull of the whole wall adds up. Gap junctions connect the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells, so that current flows from one to the next, and an action potential that starts in one cell spreads to all of them. So the atria and the ventricles each work as a functional syncytium. Once the pacemaker fires, the whole of the atria, and then the whole of the ventricles, are excited and contract together, which is what a pump needs. Skeletal muscle fibres have no intercalated discs. Each is electrically separate, and is excited at its own neuromuscular junction, by a branch of a motor neuron that supplies many other fibres too. So the nervous system can grade the force of a muscle by recruiting more or fewer motor units, and by how fast it fires them. The heart can’t do that, because every cell contracts in every beat. Its force is graded instead by how much Ca²⁺ each cell releases, and by how far the cells are stretched before they contract.

The heart sounds and the ECG

The ECG records the heart’s electrical activity from the surface of the body. The P wave comes as the atria depolarise, the QRS complex as the ventricles depolarise, and the T wave as the ventricles repolarise. The heart sounds are made as the valves close, by the vibrations set up as the blood is suddenly brought to a stop. Recording the two together shows how the electrical events lead to the mechanical ones.

The heart sounds, in the Recorder
  1. Open a recording of the heart sounds, made with an electronic stethoscope, with an ECG recorded at the same time . The start of the first sound of each beat is marked S1, and the start of the second S2.
  2. If you can, listen to it. Switch the sound on and press Play . Headphones help.
  3. Choose the Calipers and, on the heart sounds, drag from the start of an S1 to the start of the S2 that follows it. That’s systole, while the ventricles contract and eject blood.
  4. Now drag from the start of that S2 to the start of the next S1. That’s diastole, while the ventricles relax and fill.
Why does S1 come just after the QRS, and S2 near the end of the T wave? And which lasts longer at rest, systole or diastole?
S1 is heard as the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves close at the start of ventricular systole. The QRS is the ventricles depolarising, and their contraction follows that electrical activation. As soon as the pressure in the ventricles rises above the pressure in the atria, the AV valves are pushed shut. S2 is heard as the aortic and pulmonary valves close at the end of systole. The T wave is the ventricles repolarising. That ends the plateau, so Ca²⁺ stops coming in, and as the cells clear their Ca²⁺ and relax, the pressure in the ventricles falls below the pressure in the arteries and the semilunar valves close. In this recording systole lasts about 0.26 s and diastole about 0.53 s, at about 75 beats a minute, so diastole is about twice as long. As the heart rate rises, diastole shortens much more than systole. That leaves less time for the ventricles to fill, and for blood to flow through the coronary arteries of the left ventricle, which happens mostly in diastole.

Now look at the intervals of the ECG itself, in one beat of a resting 12-lead ECG.

The ECG’s intervals
  1. Open lead II of a resting 12-lead ECG, a second of it at a time . You can see a whole beat: the P wave, the QRS complex and the T wave.
  2. With the calipers, measure the PR interval. Drag from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS. The menu beside the Data pad button shows what the Recorder thinks you’ve measured, and it should say PR. If it doesn’t, move the cursors a little, or choose PR in the menu yourself and press Data pad.
  3. Then measure the QT interval, from the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave. This time the menu should say QT, and if it doesn’t, do as you did for the PR interval.
The PR interval is mostly the delay at the atrioventricular node. How long was it, and what is the delay for?
About 0.18 s (anything from 0.12 to 0.20 s is normal). The fibrous skeleton of the heart insulates the atria from the ventricles, so normally the only way for excitation to cross is through the AV node and the bundle of His. The AV node conducts slowly, and its delay makes up most of the PR interval. That gives the atria time to contract and top up the ventricles before the ventricles are excited. Then the fast His–Purkinje system spreads the excitation through the ventricles in less than a tenth of a second (the width of the QRS). Its branches run down the septum to the apex before they turn up the walls, so the ventricles contract from the apex upwards and squeeze blood towards the outflow valves.
The QT interval is about as long as a ventricular action potential. How does that compare with an action potential in skeletal muscle, and what does a long action potential do for the heart?
It was about 0.36 s here, roughly a hundred times as long as a skeletal muscle action potential, which lasts only a few milliseconds. During the plateau, Ca²⁺ coming in through L-type channels roughly balances K⁺ going out, which keeps the cell depolarised, and the Ca²⁺ that comes in triggers the release of much more from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (calcium-induced calcium release). While the cell stays depolarised its Na⁺ channels stay inactivated, so the refractory period lasts nearly as long as the contraction. Another action potential can’t start until the muscle has begun to relax, so the heart can’t be tetanised: it has to relax, and fill, between beats. Skeletal muscle, with its short action potentials and refractory periods, can add twitches together into a tetanus.

If you have time

When the atria and ventricles part company
  1. Open a recording of second-degree heart block . Some of the P waves, marked blocked P, aren’t followed by a QRS at all. From about 10 s, only every other P wave is followed by one.
In the stretch where only every other P wave is followed by a QRS, the ventricles beat at half the rate of the atria. What does that tell you about the atrioventricular node?
That it’s normally the only route by which excitation reaches the ventricles. The P waves keep their regular rhythm, because the sinoatrial node goes on setting the rate of the atria. But each time the AV node fails to conduct, no QRS follows, and the ventricles miss a beat. In a 2:1 block only every other P wave gets through. If conduction through the node failed completely (third-degree block), the ventricles would be left to a slower pacemaker of their own, at perhaps 30 to 40 beats a minute. That’s too slow for most people to manage on, which is why people with complete heart block are given artificial pacemakers.

Optional: Furchgott’s experiment

≈ 15 min

Repeat, in the Organ bath, the experiment that showed that acetylcholine relaxes arteries only if their endothelium is there.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with Dr Fraser’s second lecture, on the arterioles, and with the third short-answer question in chapter 4.

In 1980 Robert Furchgott found that acetylcholine relaxed strips of rabbit aorta only if their endothelium, the single layer of cells lining the inside of the vessel, was intact. If the endothelium had been rubbed off, acetylcholine no longer relaxed them, though noradrenaline still made them contract. The endothelium was releasing a relaxing factor, later shown to be nitric oxide (NO), and the discovery won Furchgott, Ignarro and Murad the Nobel Prize in 1998. In the Organ bath you can repeat the experiment on rings of rat aorta. Phenylephrine, an α1-adrenoceptor agonist like noradrenaline, contracts them first, so that you can see whether anything relaxes them.

A ring with its endothelium

Acetylcholine, with the endothelium
  1. Set up a ring of rat aorta . The Organ bath runs this ring 20 times faster than real time, because the aorta responds slowly, so you won’t have long to wait. This button gives everyone the same ring, so your numbers should be close to the ones in the model answers.
  2. Contract it with phenylephrine, to give 10−6 M in the bath , and let the contraction reach its plateau .
  3. Now add acetylcholine to give 10−6 M and take it to its plateau . Then wash the bath out . The Organ bath reads each response when you wash, or when you add the next drug, and puts it in the Results under the chart.

A ring without it

Acetylcholine and nitroprusside, without the endothelium
  1. Set up a second ring from the same aorta, but with its endothelium rubbed off .
  2. As before, contract it with phenylephrine and let it reach its plateau . Add acetylcholine and take that to its plateau too . Then, without washing, add sodium nitroprusside, which releases NO itself, to give 10−6 M , and take it to its plateau .
  3. Wash the bath out , and compare the three relaxations in the Results.
What do the two rings tell you about how acetylcholine relaxes the aorta?
The ring with its endothelium relaxed by about 83% of the tone that phenylephrine gave it. The rubbed ring hardly relaxed at all (about 2%). So acetylcholine doesn’t relax the smooth muscle directly. It acts on muscarinic receptors on the endothelial cells, which then make nitric oxide from L-arginine, using nitric oxide synthase. NO is small and lipophilic, so it diffuses straight into the smooth muscle. There it stimulates a soluble guanylyl cyclase to make cGMP, and the cGMP activates protein kinase G. That relaxes the muscle, by lowering its [Ca²⁺]i and making its contractile machinery less sensitive to Ca²⁺. Sodium nitroprusside relaxed the rubbed ring completely, because it releases NO itself. The muscle could still respond to NO, so what the ring had lost was the ability to make it.
Sildenafil (Viagra) makes the NO pathway more effective. How?

If you have time

Acetylcholine on its own
  1. Once the rubbed ring has settled after the wash , add acetylcholine to it on its own, at a high concentration, 10−4 M , and take it to its plateau . Watch the chart rather than the Results, which measure acetylcholine’s responses as relaxations.
Furchgott saw this too: at high concentrations, acetylcholine made the rubbed preparations contract. What does that suggest about where its receptors are?
That the smooth muscle has muscarinic receptors of its own, and that they make it contract, as muscarinic receptors on smooth muscle do elsewhere (in the ileum in Supervision 2, for example). Here the ring contracted by about half a gram. In a ring with its endothelium, this is hidden by the much stronger relaxation that acetylcholine causes through the endothelium and NO. So the same transmitter can relax a blood vessel or contract it, depending on which of its cells have the receptors.
Flow through a vessel goes with the fourth power of its radius (Poiseuille’s law). If an arteriole constricts so that its radius falls by 16%, what fraction of the flow is left? Work out 0.84⁴, on a calculator or in R.
About half: 0.84⁴ = 0.50. The other way round, doubling the radius would increase the flow 16 times (2⁴), and the fourfold range of diameters that arterioles can span changes the flow about 250 times (4⁴ = 256). That’s why the arterioles control the flow through each tissue, and why the balance between dilators such as NO and constrictors such as noradrenaline matters so much.

“Explain how the structural and electrical properties of cardiac muscle enable coordinated, rhythmic pumping of blood while allowing relaxation between beats.”

Thinking about the title

The title asks how the properties of cardiac muscle make three things possible. The pumping has to be coordinated, with the atria contracting before the ventricles, and all the cells of each contracting together. It has to be rhythmic, with a beat that starts itself, at a rate that can change. And the heart has to relax between beats, so that it can fill. A good essay is organised around those demands, and shows which structural and electrical properties meet each one, rather than describing the heart from top to bottom. These questions might help you plan. Three of them take the demands in turn, and the other two, on pumping and on the whole cycle, help you tie them to the heart as a pump.

  • Coordinated. How do the junctions between the cells, and the conduction system, make the atria and then the ventricles contract as units, and in the right order? Chapter 1 has some numbers for this.
  • Rhythmic. Where does each beat start, what makes the pacemaker cells fire on their own, and how do the autonomic nerves change the rate?
  • Pumping. How does an action potential become a contraction in a cardiac muscle cell, and how is the force of a beat graded, when every cell contracts every time?
  • Relaxation. What stops the heart from being tetanised, and what has to happen for it to relax and fill? Why does relaxing need energy?
  • The whole cycle. How do the valves, which open and close passively, turn these contractions into one-way flow?

Comparing cardiac muscle with skeletal muscle, which you wrote about last week, can help, but only where the comparison explains something about the heart.
