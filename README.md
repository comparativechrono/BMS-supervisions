# BMS supervisions

**Supervisions for NST IA Biomedical Sciences with Tim Hearn, built on the [BMS workbench](https://github.com/comparativechrono/BMS-workbench).** The landing page has a tile for each of the eight supervisions. Each supervision's page holds the work to hand in – an essay, written in the workbench's *Supervision work* tab as students will write in the online exam, then handed in from the same page, as a Word document, straight to the supervisor's Google Drive – and short exercises in the workbench, with the instructions alongside: a virtual microscope, the human body in 3D, real recordings of people, an organ bath, flow diagrams that run, circuits, PhET simulations, molecules in 3D and RStudio.

Everything runs in the student's web browser. Students don't need to install anything, there is no server-side code, and their files, answers and essays are stored in their own browser. The site is static (HTML, CSS, JavaScript and the data: slides, models, recordings and simulations), so it can be hosted for free on **GitHub Pages**.

**Supervision 1** – feedback, hormones and the hypothalamus, seen through the body's daily clock – is ready. The tiles of Supervisions 2 to 8 open the workbench and its guide until their work is added (see [Adding a supervision](#adding-a-supervision)).

## The site

| Page | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `index.html` | The landing page: a tile for each supervision, showing how far the student has got with its exercises and what they have done with its essay (started, submitted, handed in), and the student's name, which goes on the work they hand in (every page of the site shares it) |
| `supervision-1.html` | Supervision 1: its chapters on the left and the workbench on the right, opening on Supervision work |
| `workbench.html` | The workbench's guide: a tour of its tabs (the BMS workbench's starter practical). The tiles of the supervisions whose work isn't ready yet open it |

Each supervision's page is the workbench, with that supervision's chapters. Its first tab, **Supervision work** (the BMS workbench's *Exam practice*, renamed and moved to the front), lists the supervision's own papers first – the work to hand in, marked *To hand in*, then the optional papers – then the practice papers, and, closed, the other supervisions' papers. A paper to hand in has no time limit but a suggested time: its clock counts the time spent writing up to the suggested time, says when it is reached, and locks nothing. **Handing in is built in**, as at the end of an exam. After submitting, the student types the hand-in password that the supervisor gives them (it is not on the site) and presses *Hand it in*. The page sends the Word document – named after the paper and the student, with their name at the top – to the hand-in script, which runs in Tim Hearn's Google account (`handIn.script` in `config.js`; [handin/README.md](handin/README.md) says how it was set up): it checks the password, saves the document in his Google Drive (a folder for each supervision), adds a line to a Google Sheet that lists the work handed in, and sends back a receipt, which the page shows. Students don't need a Google account. If the essay can't be handed in, the page says why, and what to do. The paper's first page offers to hand it in later (or again), and the list of papers says whether each piece of work has been handed in. For the landing page, the site remembers when each supervision's paper to hand in was started, submitted, downloaded and handed in.

Everything the site saves has its own prefix (`storePrefix`, `bmssup`), so the students' files, ticks and answers here are kept apart from the BMS workbench's, although both are at `comparativechrono.github.io`. Each page keeps its ticks and answers under its own name (its `data-page`), and the pages share the student's home folder and their name.

## Supervision 1

*Feedback, hormones and the hypothalamus*: the week's lectures (homeostasis and feedback, moving things around, hormones, the hypothalamus and pituitary, and the adrenal gland), seen through the body's daily clock.

**To hand in:** an essay, *Discuss, with examples, why negative feedback mechanisms are more common than positive feedback mechanisms in animal physiology* (`exams/s1-essay.json`). It has no time limit but suggests about 40 minutes; pasting text copied from elsewhere is refused, as in the exam; and when it is submitted the student hands it in from the same page, with the password that Tim Hearn gives them, and it goes straight to his Google Drive. The essay's first page explains why it is written like this. **Optional:** the week's five short-answer questions (`exams/s1-saqs.json`) and four more essay titles for revision (`exams/s1-revision.json`), each with guidance that students see after they submit.

## Adding a supervision

1. **Its papers.** One JSON file for each paper, in `exams/`, named `sN-….json` and with `"week": N`. The paper to hand in has `"required": true`, `"minutes": null` (no time limit), `"suggestedMinutes": 40` and `"handIn": true`, which hands it in the way that `config.js` sets up (`handIn`). Optional papers leave out `required` and `handIn`. Copy `exams/s1-essay.json` to start.
2. **Its chapters.** A module `build/sN.py` with a function `ALL()` that returns the chapters, as in `build/s1.py`, using the helpers of `build/lib.py` (see [Writing chapters](#writing-chapters)). Give the chapters, steps and questions ids of their own (`sN-…`, checked when the page is built), and put the extras inside `optional()`, so that they don't count towards finishing the page.
3. **Its data**, if it has any: files in `starter/` are copied into each student's home folder (increase `STARTERS_VERSION` in `build/make_page.py` when you add files after students have started), and diagrams and circuits go in `examples/`.
4. **Its entry** in `build/supervisions.py`: set its `status` to `'ready'`, and give its `page` (`supervision-N.html`), `data_page`, `module`, `title`, `topic`, `subtitle`, `description`, the texts of its tile (`card`, `handin`, `handin_short`, `time`) and `handin_paper` (the id of its paper to hand in).
5. Run `python3 build/make_page.py`, which writes `supervision-N.html` and the landing page; try it with `python3 serve.py`; and test it as a student would use it (`test/t_s1.py` does this for Supervision 1).

## What's in the workbench

Each supervision's page, and the guide, is the workbench. The **instructions** are on the left: chapters made up of steps that tick themselves off as the student completes them, and questions with model answers (some of which check a numerical answer). The **workbench** itself is on the right, with eleven tabs. Students can hide the instructions (with the button at the top left, or <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>\</kbd>) so that the workbench fills the screen. On a phone or a tablet, the workbench opens on top of the instructions (the **Workbench** button), and ✕ at the right of its tabs goes back to them. When there isn't room for all the tabs' names, they are set in smaller type, and when even that doesn't fit, the tabs show only their icons and the open tab keeps its name (on the smallest phones, the open tab is shown by its colour). The page's **Help** shows which icon is which tab.

| Tab | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| **Microscope** | A virtual light microscope. The **slide box** holds 31 slides, sorted by the system of the body: 29 sections of human tissue stained with H&E, from the GTEx project (normal tissues, except a coronary artery with atherosclerosis), and two slides drawn for practising measurement (a stage micrometer and a haemocytometer with living and dead cells). **Objectives** from 1× to 40× (keys 1 to 6) follow the convention of digital microscopy – 20× shows a slide at the full resolution of its scan, 0.5 µm per screen pixel – with a scale bar, a readout of the magnification and the field of view, and a map of the whole slide. The **eyepiece view** shows a round field of view with a graticule of 100 divisions, which students calibrate against the stage micrometer. The tools **measure** lengths and areas, **count** cells in up to six categories, and add **labels**; the camera takes **pictures** with a scale bar and the slide's credit, which keep their scale when they are opened again. Many slides carry teaching **labels** (hidden at first, so that students look for themselves), and the instructions can ask students to **find** a labelled structure and click on it (*Show me* takes them to an example and rings it). Every measurement and count is kept in `~/microscope/measurements.csv` and `~/microscope/counts.csv`, ready for R, and the microscope opens a ready-made R script for them. Students can also open a picture of their own (a photograph through a microscope, say) as a slide, and give it a scale from its scale bar. |
| **Recorder** | A chart recorder for real recordings of people, from the open-access databases of PhysioNet: 14 excerpts – the ECG (sinus rhythm, a 12-lead ECG, ventricular ectopic beats, the onset of atrial fibrillation, second-degree heart block), heart sounds with the ECG, blood pressure on standing up, the pulse (PPG) with breathing, heart rate and breathing (respiratory sinus arrhythmia), heart rate during exercise, needle EMG (healthy, myopathy, neuropathy) and the EEG with the eyes open and closed. Stacked channels on one time axis, zoom and scroll (and an overview of the whole recording), **Play** to replay a recording in real time as if it were being recorded (with sound for the heart sounds and the EMG), two-cursor **calipers** (Δt, the rate 60 ÷ Δt per minute and in Hz, the change and the lowest, highest and mean value of a channel), a marker, a **heart-rate channel** worked out beat by beat, the beat labels and rhythms given by cardiologists, a **data pad** of measurements – each an R–R, PR, QRS, QT interval or other, with the heart rate from the R–R intervals – kept as `~/recorder/measurements.csv`, CSV export of a recording (and its beats) for R, scripts for R, and pictures. |
| **Organ bath** | Isolated-tissue pharmacology, simulated: a length of guinea-pig ileum or a ring of rat aorta (with or without its endothelium) in an organ bath, drawn with its transducer, aeration and water jacket. Students add drugs by volume from stock solutions (the bath concentration is worked out exactly, C<sub>stock</sub> × *v* / (*V* + *v*), and cumulative doses add up), watch the tension on a chart-recorder trace with the doses marked under it, wash out, and let time run at ×1, ×5 or ×20 (or skip to the plateau, or wait 10 minutes for an antagonist to equilibrate). Each response is measured automatically, when the bath is washed or the next drug is added, into a **Results** table – kept as `~/organbath/<tissue>.csv` – and **Analyse in R** writes and opens a script that fits Hill curves with `nls()`, works out EC<sub>50</sub>s, dose ratios and pA<sub>2</sub>, and draws a Schild plot. Agonists, a partial agonist, competitive, non-competitive and irreversible antagonists, an anticholinesterase, nicotine and a ganglion blocker, nitric oxide and its synthase, KCl, and two unknown drugs to identify. The responses come from the operational model of agonism with noise, kinetics and differences between tissues: each fresh tissue has a random seed of its own, so every student's tissues (and unknown drugs) differ, and a button in the instructions can give a seed so that everyone has the same one ([build/organbath/README.md](build/organbath/README.md)). It is a teaching simulation, not data from real tissues. |
| **Diagrams** | Flow diagrams of physiological control: boxes for the quantities, stimuli, set points and comparators, and arrows that stimulate (+) or inhibit (−), each with a strength and a delay. **Run** turns the diagram into a simple model and plots how each quantity changes with time, so that students can see what negative and positive feedback, feed-forward and delays do. Boxes can be knocked out or held fixed. Diagrams are saved in `~/diagrams` (`.flow.json`) and can be exported as pictures (PNG or SVG) and their results as a table for R (CSV). Examples: feedback in temperature control, feed-forward, positive feedback in clotting, a delayed loop that oscillates, blood glucose, and a template for an endocrine axis. |
| **Circuits** | An electronics bench: batteries, current sources, AC sources, resistors, capacitors, inductors, switches, diodes, voltmeters and ammeters, joined by wires, with a simulator that works out the voltages and currents as time passes, and traces that plot them. In **Physiology** mode the same parts become pressure and flow sources, resistances to flow, compliances, valves, pressure gauges and flowmeters (in mmHg and mL/s). Examples include Ohm's law, a voltage divider, resistances in series and in parallel, charging a capacitor, the membrane as an RC circuit, the parallel-conductance model of the resting membrane potential and the Windkessel model of the circulation. Circuits are saved in `~/circuits` (`.circuit.json`), and can be exported as pictures and their traces as CSV. |
| **Simulations** | A library of 13 **PhET Interactive Simulations** (University of Colorado Boulder) chosen for biomedical science – Neuron, Membrane Transport, Diffusion, Gas Properties, Under Pressure, pH Scale, Acid-Base Solutions, Molarity, Beer's Law Lab, Color Vision, Geometric Optics, Capacitor Lab: Basics and Least-Squares Regression – kept with the site in the versions PhET published under CC BY 4.0, and run in the tab with PhET's usage statistics switched off. Search and topics, each simulation's screens, full screen, and an About box with what it is for, its authors, its version and its licence. |
| **Supervision work** | The work set for the supervisions, written as in the online exam system (Inspera), so that students get used to writing in a browser before they have to. It lists the page's own supervision's papers first (the one to hand in at the top), then the practice papers and the other supervisions'. A paper has a start page, a timer – or, with no time limit, a clock that counts the time spent writing up to a suggested time – one question at a time with an essay editor (paragraph styles, bold, italic, underline, sub- and superscript, lists, indents, tables, special characters, formulas, drawings, pictures from the student's computer – a photo of a diagram drawn on paper, say – undo and backups), a word count with a limit, flags, question tiles, a calculator, resources (which can open slides in the microscope), a submission page, a receipt, and a review with the marking guidance. As in Inspera's exam browser, only what was copied in the exam can be pasted into it: text copied from anywhere else is refused. Answers save themselves as students type (in `~/essays`), and a submission can be downloaded as a **Word document**, as a PDF (by printing), as a web page or as text. Papers are JSON files (`exams/`), and *Write an essay* starts a free essay for any title. A paper to hand in says how on its first page and after it is submitted, and puts the student's name on its Word document. |
| **Anatomy** | The human body in 3D, from **Z-Anatomy** (CC BY-SA 4.0; most of its models made from BodyParts3D): 12 systems (skeleton, muscles, heart and great vessels, arteries and veins, respiratory, digestive, urinary, male reproductive, endocrine glands, brain, spinal cord with nerves and eyes, lymphoid organs) and 2214 structures, each with its English name, nearly all (1993) with their Latin name and TA2 number, and four in five (1802) with the opening of a Wikipedia article. Turn the body round and zoom; show, hide or fade each system; click a structure to select it, then isolate it, fade the others round it or hide it; standard views (anterior, posterior, left, right, superior, inferior); sagittal, coronal and transverse sections with a slider (transverse ones seen from below, as CT and MRI show them); labels in the manner of an atlas; *find* tasks ("Find the thyroid gland and click on it"); a search by English, Latin or everyday names (*adrenal*, *windpipe*); pictures saved in Files (`~/anatomy`). ## Before students use it

1. **Computers and browsers.** Students need a laptop or desktop computer with a current version of Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari; 8 GB of memory is comfortable. The workbench also works on a phone, although the screen is cramped (the simulations are best with the phone turned sideways). The Anatomy and Molecules tabs need WebGL (3D graphics), which almost every computer has.
2. **Network.** Students' browsers need to reach your site (the page and the slides) and, for the tabs that use them: `webr.r-wasm.org` (R itself, about 30 MB the first time RStudio is opened, then kept in the browser's cache) and `repo.r-wasm.org` (R packages, when they are installed); `models.rcsb.org` and `files.rcsb.org` (structures from the Protein Data Bank that are not in the library) and `alphafold.ebi.ac.uk` (AlphaFold models). The Microscope, Anatomy, Recorder, Organ bath, Diagrams, Circuits, Simulations, Supervision work and the library of the Molecules tab only need your site.
3. **Shared computers.** Students' files and essays are stored in the browser profile, so computers that wipe profiles when students log out will lose them. Tell students to download their work before they leave (*My files → Download all my files*, *My answers → Download my answers*, and *Download* on each submitted essay), and to use *Start afresh* when they finish on a shared computer.
4. **Supervision work is practice.** It works like the candidate's view of an online exam system, but it is not one: it is not locked down, it sends nothing to a server but the work that is handed in, and the student can see the model answers after submitting. Use it for practice and formative work, not for assessed exams. Whether the browser's spelling checker is on at the start is set for each paper (it is off by default, as it usually is in exams); students can switch it.
5. **Personal and patient data.** The slides are public, de-identified research images. If you add your own slides or pictures, make sure that you may publish them (anything on the site is public).
6. **Handing in.** The hand-in script is set up ([handin/README.md](handin/README.md)) and its address is in `config.js` (`handIn.script`): give the students the hand-in password. For a new year, change the password in the script's settings.

## Publishing it on GitHub Pages

1. On GitHub, open the repository's *Settings → Pages → Build and deployment* page. Under Source choose **Deploy from a branch**, then choose the branch **main** and the folder **/ (root)**, and click Save. (The repository needs to be public, unless your GitHub plan supports Pages for private repositories.)
2. After a minute or two, the site will be available at `https://<owner>.github.io/<repository>/`, which for this repository is `https://comparativechrono.github.io/BMS-supervisions/`. That is the address to give to students: it opens the landing page.
3. The first time a browser opens the site, the page reloads itself once. This installs a small service worker (`isolation-sw.js`, next to `index.html`), which makes the page *cross-origin isolated*, so that R can wait for typed input and be interrupted with <kbd>Esc</kbd>; it also serves the Shiny apps that R runs.

**Size and bandwidth.** The site is about 415 MB: the slides take 360 MB (`slides/`, about 12 MB each), the simulations 33 MB (`sims/`, 1–6 MB each), the anatomy models 10 MB and the recordings 2 MB. Each is downloaded only when a student opens it. GitHub Pages allows a published site of up to 1 GB and has a soft limit of 100 GB of traffic a month. A student who looks at a slide only downloads the parts they look at, at the magnifications they use (the microscope asks for each tile of the slide with an HTTP *range request* into the slide's `tiles.pack` file, which GitHub Pages and most web servers answer), so a practical with a few slides usually costs a few megabytes per student. If you add many more slides, keep an eye on the size of the repository (GitHub recommends staying under 1 GB) or host the slides elsewhere (see `slideBase` below).

The empty file `.nojekyll` stops GitHub from processing the site. The site itself doesn't need the `build/` and `test/` folders, but do keep `assets/vendor/licenses/`. To host the workbench on a different web server, just copy the folder (you can leave out `build/` and `test/`); the server must answer range requests (almost all do), and the address must use `https` (or be `localhost`) for the service worker to work.

**Students' files belong to the site's address.** The browser stores them for that address, so if the workbench moves to a different address, students will start with an empty home folder there. All the GitHub Pages sites of one account share the same address (`https://<owner>.github.io`), which matters when you have more than one practical: see `storePrefix` below.

## Trying it on your own computer

Opening `index.html` directly from your disk (`file://…`) doesn't work, because browsers block the Web Workers and the data files. Instead, serve the folder with a local web server:

```bash
cd BMS-supervisions
python3 serve.py 8000        # then open http://localhost:8000
```

`serve.py` answers range requests (which the microscope needs) and sends the cross-origin isolation headers itself. Python's own `python3 -m http.server` does **not** answer range requests, so the microscope only works with it after downloading each slide in full.

## Writing chapters

The supervisions' chapters are written like the BMS workbench's practicals. [Adding a supervision](#adding-a-supervision) says which files to change; this section describes the chapters themselves. Supervision 1 (`build/s1.py`) is a model for most of what a supervision's page does: a hand-in box, buttons that open diagrams, slides, parts of the body, molecules, recordings and papers, R typed into the Console for the student, multiple-choice questions with feedback, model answers, and extras that don't count.

The chapters, the papers' instructions and guidance and the landing page's notes are written in the supervisor's own voice, in the first person ("the hand-in password I give you", "my Google Drive"), in full sentences and British English. The site's own messages, in Supervision work and in the tiles, name the supervisor instead, from `handIn.to` in `config.js`.

### The chapters

The chapters are written in Python – the guide's in `build/chapters.py`, each supervision's in `build/sN.py` – using the helper functions in `build/lib.py`. Running `python3 build/make_page.py` puts them into the pages (between the `<!--CHAPTERS-->` and `<!--/CHAPTERS-->` markers in `build/template.html`), writes the landing page from `build/landing.html`, and also writes the lists that the tabs read (`slides/index.json`, `exams/index.json` and `examples/index.json`). It reports an error (and exits with an error status, after writing the pages) if two tasks, questions or headings of a page have the same name. Each module's `ALL()` lists its chapters in order. A chapter can also be kept in a file of its own, `build/ch_<name>.py`, with a function `chapter(num)` that returns it, as the chapters of the Anatomy, Recorder, Organ bath and Simulations tabs are (inside such a file, call the helper as `lib.chapter(...)`, since the file's own function is called `chapter` too).

## Slides, models, recordings, simulations, papers and examples

**Slides** live in `slides/<id>/` (an image pyramid of WebP tiles packed into one file, with `slide.json` describing it); `build/make_page.py` lists them in `slides/index.json`, sorted by system. [build/slides/README.md](build/slides/README.md) explains how the GTEx slides were chosen and made, how to label a slide for *find* tasks, and how to add a slide of your own (`build/slides/add_image.py`, from a photograph with a known scale or from a slide scanner's file).

**The models of the Anatomy tab** are in `anatomy/`: one glTF file for each part of the body (the skeleton, the muscles, the heart, the vessels…), `index.json`, which lists the 2214 structures (their ids, English and Latin names, TA2 numbers, systems and groups), and `definitions.json`. They were made from Z-Anatomy's Blender file by the scripts in `build/anatomy/` ([build/anatomy/README.md](build/anatomy/README.md) explains how, what was simplified and what was left out, and why); `build/anatomy/config.json` says which structures go into which file. A *find* task or a *show me* button names a structure by its id in `index.json`.

**The recordings of the Recorder** are in `recordings/`: for each, a `.json` file (its channels, units, sampling rate, annotations, where it comes from and how to cite it) and a `.bin` file (the samples, as 16-bit numbers), listed in `recordings/index.json`. They are excerpts of PhysioNet's open-access databases, made by `build/recordings/make_recordings.py` from the list in `build/recordings/recordings.json` (the script checks each download against the database's checksums); [build/recordings/README.md](build/recordings/README.md) gives each recording's source, licence, citation and what was changed. To add a recording, add an entry to `recordings.json` (only from a database whose licence allows it) and run the script. `recordingBase` in `config.js` lets another site hold them.

**The simulations** are in `sims/`, one folder for each (PhET's file, unchanged, and a picture), listed in `sims/index.json`, which `build/sims/fetch_sims.py` writes from `build/sims/sims.json`. Each is pinned to the last version PhET published under CC BY 4.0: **don't replace a simulation with PhET's latest version**, which is licensed CC BY-NC (see [build/sims/README.md](build/sims/README.md)). To add a simulation, add it to `sims.json` with the version to use, and run `fetch_sims.py`, which refuses a file that doesn't carry the CC BY 4.0 licence.

**The organ bath** has no data files: its tissues, drugs and their values are in `assets/js/organbath.js`, and [build/organbath/README.md](build/organbath/README.md) gives the model, the values and where they come from.

**Papers of Supervision work** are JSON files in `exams/` (the format is described at the top of `assets/js/exam.js`): a title, the time allowed (or `"minutes": null` and `suggestedMinutes`: no limit, and a suggested time), instructions, resources (the calculator, slides, links) and questions – information pages, essays (with a word limit, which can be a hard limit), short answers, numerical questions (with units and a tolerance) and multiple-choice questions – each with marking guidance that students see when they review their submitted answers. Pasting is kept within the exam unless a paper says `"clipboard": "any"` (as an exam taken in an ordinary browser allows). A supervision's papers have `week` (its number); the one to hand in also has `required: true` and `handIn`: `true` to hand it in through the hand-in script that `config.js` sets up, an object for a hand-in of its own (`{"script": …, "to": …, "password": true}`, which adds to `config.js`'s), or HTML that only says how to hand it in. The site comes with Supervision 1's three papers and the BMS workbench's three practice papers (*Getting to know exam practice*, *Practice essay (45 minutes)* and *Calculations*). Write a new paper for each essay you set, and open it from the chapters with `exam('its-id')`.

**Examples of diagrams and circuits** are the `.flow.json` and `.circuit.json` files in `examples/diagrams/` and `examples/circuits/`, offered in each tab's Examples menu. The easiest way to make one is to build it in the tab, save it, download it from Files, and copy it into the folder (give it a `title`, a `description`, a `group` and an `order`). Supervision 1 added the circadian clock (`clock.flow.json`, run for 240 hours so that light has time to entrain it) and thirst (`thirst.flow.json`).

**The Molecules tab's library** is in `molecules/` (BinaryCIF files of the proteins, SDF files of the small molecules), and the list is in `assets/js/viewer-bms.js`. [molecules/README.md](molecules/README.md) says where they came from and how to add one.

## What students keep

Students' files, ticks, answers, essays and settings are stored in the browser's storage for the site (IndexedDB and local storage) on their own computer, and they are still there when the student comes back in the same browser. Nothing is stored on a server, and nobody else can see them. A different browser, a different computer or a private window won't have them, and clearing the browser's data for the site deletes them. *My files → Download all my files* downloads everything as a `.zip` file, *My answers → Download my answers* downloads the answers as a web page, and each essay can be downloaded as a Word document. The page warns students if it is open in two tabs of the same browser, because both would write to the same files.

## Privacy

The site sets no cookies and has no tracking or analytics. The only things that leave a student's computer are requests for the page and its slides, models, recordings and simulations (from your server); R and its packages (from `webr.r-wasm.org` and `repo.r-wasm.org`), when RStudio is used; structures that are not in the library (from RCSB PDB and AlphaFold DB), when the student asks for them; and anything the student's own R code requests; and the work that a student hands in. The page sends it to the hand-in script in Tim Hearn's Google account – the Word document, the student's name (and candidate number, if they gave one), the paper, its number of words, when it was submitted, and the hand-in password, which the page doesn't keep – and the script keeps the document, and a line about it, in his Google Drive. Shiny apps run inside the page. The PhET simulations are opened with PhET's usage statistics switched off (`?yotta=false`), so they contact neither PhET nor Google.

## Browser support and limitations

The workbench works in current versions of Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari. It was tested in Chromium; please try the browsers your students use, particularly Safari.

- **The slides** were scanned with a 20× objective (about 0.49 µm per pixel), so 40× enlarges the scan without showing finer detail, and fine structures that need oil immersion (bacteria, the fine structure of chromosomes) can't be seen. The slides are regions of a few millimetres cut from each GTEx section, not the whole section. They are sections of normal tissue (apart from the coronary artery, which has atherosclerosis), mostly from donors after death, so some show the changes of autolysis or of age (the slide's *About* panel gives the GTEx pathologist's notes).
- **The Anatomy tab**'s body is a man's (BodyParts3D was made from one man), so there are no female reproductive organs. The ear, the inside of the kidney and the cerebral white matter are left out because of their licences, and the teeth, joints, skin, meninges, pleura and peritoneum to keep the models small and clear. Four structures in five (1802 of 2214) have a definition. The models are of one man, so some organs lie where his did rather than where textbooks put them: his thyroid gland sits high, on the larynx (the chapter's question says so). A section shows the inner surfaces of the organs it cuts (the cut faces are not filled), and the thinnest nerves and vessels are easier to find with the search box than by clicking.
- **The recordings** are short excerpts (13 seconds to 10 minutes) of PhysioNet's databases, with the annotations those databases give. In five of them (the 12-lead ECG, atrial fibrillation, the heart sounds, the blood pressure on standing and the pulse), the beats were found by a detector when the recordings were made and checked by eye, not by a cardiologist (their *About* panels say so). The ECG during exercise and both channels of the heart sounds are in arbitrary units (their databases give no calibration); in the atrial fibrillation recording (an ambulatory recorder's leads) the P waves are small and the fibrillation waves too small to see, so the R–R intervals carry the contrast; and the EEG's eyes-open and eyes-closed parts are two recordings put side by side (the Recorder marks the join). Sound plays at the chosen speed, so ×2 raises its pitch.
- **The organ bath** is a teaching simulation: its responses come from a model with typical, rounded values (with noise and differences between tissues), not from real tissues, and only nicotine desensitises. It has two preparations (the guinea-pig ileum and the rat aorta), and the unknown drugs are for the ileum only.
- **The simulations** are PhET's versions from before 29 March 2026 (the last under CC BY 4.0), so they won't receive PhET's later fixes; they are the English versions only. PhET designs them for a wide screen, so on a phone they are best turned sideways. A few steps of the starter practical tick themselves from values inside the simulations, which a new version could rename (the tests check them).
- **Handing in.** The hand-in script is the supervisor's own: it runs in his Google account, within Google's quotas for Apps Script, and hands in one essay at a time (if many arrive at once, a student may be asked to try again in a moment). Anyone with its address and the password could hand something in, so give the password only to the students, and change it each year. Every hand-in is kept (nothing is replaced) and listed in the sheet, with the student's name.
- **Supervision work** imitates the candidate's view of an online exam system; the real system may differ in its details, and it changes from year to year. It runs entirely in the browser: there is no lockdown, invigilation or plagiarism check (the page refuses pastes and drops of text copied elsewhere, but nothing stops a student retyping it), the clock is the computer's own, and the answers and marking guidance of a paper are in its file in `exams/` (so a determined student can read them). Formulas go into Word documents as text with sub- and superscripts, not as equation objects, and the spelling checker is the browser's own. Pictures are made smaller in the browser – at most 1600 pixels on their longer side, and 700 KB – and an answer can have up to 10 of them; a photo in the HEIC format of iPhones doesn't open in every browser, so it may need saving as a JPEG first (an iPhone itself usually sends a JPEG when a picture is chosen in a web page). The page says so when a picture can't be opened.
- **R in the browser** is R 4.6 (webR 0.6.0): one thread, a few gigabytes of memory, packages from the webR repository (most of CRAN, including ggplot2 and dplyr), and documents knitted to HTML only. RStudio's tab is an imitation made for teaching.
- **Cross-origin isolation.** R can only wait for typed input and be interrupted with <kbd>Esc</kbd> if the page is cross-origin isolated. GitHub Pages can't send the headers for this, so `assets/js/isolation.js` registers `isolation-sw.js`, a service worker that adds them (it doesn't cache anything). On a student's first visit, the page reloads itself once. Adding `?isolation=off` to the address removes the service worker.
