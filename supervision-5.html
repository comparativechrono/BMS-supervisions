Optional: exercise and standing up

≈ 15 min

Measure, in the Recorder, how the heart rate climbs through ten minutes of cycling, and how the baroreflex brings the blood pressure back when someone stands up.

Optional, about 15 minutes

Ten minutes of cycling

This recording is an ECG from the chest of a volunteer who cycled against a high resistance for ten minutes. Under the ECG is his heart rate, worked out from the beats.

The heart rate in sustained exercise
  1. Open the first few seconds of the recording .
  2. Choose the Calipers and drag from the peak of one R wave to the peak of the next. The bar above the traces shows the interval (Δt) and the heart rate it gives.
  3. Now go to the last few seconds of the ten minutes , and measure an R–R interval there too.
How much did his heart rate rise? Pacing a resting heart this much faster would hardly change its output. Why does the same rise in rate help in exercise? Use CO = HR × SV and VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR.
It rose by about half, from about 84 beats a minute at the start (an R–R interval of about 0.71 s) to about 130 at the end (0.46 s), and it was still rising after ten minutes. CO = HR × SV, so a faster heart raises the output only if each beat still ejects as much blood. Pace a resting heart faster and it doesn’t. Venous return is set by VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR, and RAP is already close to zero, so the extra beats can’t draw much more blood back. Each beat fills less, SV falls and CO hardly changes. In exercise, venous return rises at the same time, because sympathetic venoconstriction raises MSFP and the muscle pump lowers RvR. The faster heart, made more forceful by sympathetic stimulation through β1-adrenoceptors, pumps that extra blood without letting RAP rise. So SV is kept up, or even rises, as HR rises, and CO rises several-fold.

Standing up

When you stand up, about half a litre of blood pools in the veins of your legs. In this recording a healthy young man stood up from lying on a tilt table, with his blood pressure measured beat by beat at a finger, and an ECG.

The baroreflex, in the Recorder
  1. Open the recording, from 16 s before he stands up to 34 s after . A flag marks the moment he stands up, and the heart rate is at the bottom.
  2. With the calipers, drag across one beat of the blood pressure from before he stands up, from the lowest point of one pulse to the lowest point of the next. Choose a beat before 37 s, before he starts to move. The bar above the traces now shows the minimum pressure (the diastolic), the maximum (the systolic) and the mean.
  3. Do the same for a beat at the lowest pressure, a few seconds after he stands up.
Use the minimum and maximum of your beat from before he stood up to estimate his mean arterial pressure, with MAP ≈ D + (S − D) / 3. How close is your estimate to the mean that the Recorder measured? And why is the mean nearer to the diastolic pressure than to the systolic?
For a beat at about 33 s, the pressure was about 97/53 mmHg. The pulse pressure was about 44 mmHg, so MAP ≈ 53 + 44/3 ≈ 68 mmHg. The Recorder measured a mean of about 65 mmHg, so the estimate was within about 3 mmHg (your beat will give slightly different numbers). The mean is nearer to the diastolic pressure because the pressure spends more of each cycle near it. It rises quickly to a brief peak while the ventricle ejects, then falls slowly through diastole, which is the longer part of the cycle at rest. The one-third rule is only an approximation, and how well it works depends on the shape of the pressure wave.
Which term of ABP = CO × TPR fell first when he stood up, and how did his body bring the pressure back? Use your numbers, and the heart rate.
Cardiac output. Within a few seconds his mean pressure fell from about 65 to about 51 mmHg (a beat of about 84/38 mmHg). Standing doesn’t lower TPR. It pools blood in the veins of the legs, so it’s harder for blood to get back to the heart, venous return and RAP fall, and by Starling’s law each beat ejects less. With CO lower, ABP = CO × TPR falls. The baroreceptors of the carotid sinus and aortic arch then fire less, so the medulla reduces vagal activity to the heart and raises sympathetic activity. His heart rate rose from about 77 to about 105 a minute within about ten seconds. Sympathetic nerves also constrict the arterioles, which raises TPR, and the veins, which raises MSFP and so venous return. His pressure was back about ten seconds after he stood up. It then overshot, to about 130/70 mmHg, and his heart rate fell back for a few beats as the baroreflex now worked the other way, before both settled, with his heart rate higher than when he was lying down.

Optional: exercise in a model of the circulation

≈ 15 min

Run Dr Fraser’s two equations as a model of the circulation in the Circuits tab, and find out what has to change for the cardiac output to rise five-fold.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with Dr Fraser’s third and fifth lectures, and it gives you numbers you could use in your essay.

Dr Fraser’s two equations make a model of the circulation that you can run. In it, the mean systemic filling pressure (MSFP) is a pressure source, which drives venous return to the right atrium through the resistance to venous return (RvR), so that VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR. The heart is a flow source, which pumps the flow you set into the arteries. From there the blood flows back through the arterioles, the total peripheral resistance (TPR), so that ABP = CO × TPR. The Circuits tab shows pressures in mmHg, flows in mL/s (5 L/min is 83.3 mL/s) and resistances in mmHg·s/mL.

A model, not a body

The veins of this model are rigid, so RAP can fall as far as the heart pulls it. Real veins collapse once the pressure inside them falls 1 or 2 mmHg below zero, so in the body RAP can’t fall much below zero. A real heart also sets its own output. By Starling’s law it pumps what returns to it, which keeps RAP close to zero. Here you set the heart’s output yourself.

At rest

The circulation at rest
  1. Open the model and run it . The two flowmeters show that venous return and cardiac output are both 83.3 mL/s (5 L/min). In a steady circulation they have to be equal. RAP is close to 0 mmHg, and ABP is 93 mmHg.

Exercise

Five times the cardiac output
  1. Start exercising. Click the switch Exercising muscles to close it. That opens the dilated arterioles of the working muscles beside the others, and TPR falls to a fifth of its resting value. Watch ABP.
  2. Can the heart put this right on its own? Click the Heart, and in the panel on the right, set its flow to 417 mL/s (25 L/min, five times its resting output).
When the muscles’ arterioles dilated, ABP fell to about 19 mmHg. Why? And with the heart pumping 417 mL/s, ABP is back to 93 mmHg. What has happened to RAP, and why couldn’t a real heart do this?
With CO unchanged, ABP = CO × TPR fell to a fifth, from 93 to about 19 mmHg, because TPR fell to a fifth. Pumping five times as much restores ABP, but RAP falls to about −28 mmHg. With MSFP and RvR unchanged, VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR can only rise five-fold if RAP falls far below zero: (7 + 28) / 0.084 = 417 mL/s. Real veins collapse once the pressure in them falls 1 or 2 mmHg below zero, so venous return would stop rising long before that. A heart that pumps harder can only lower RAP, and RAP is already close to zero at rest, so the heart can’t pull more blood out of the veins. That’s why pacing the heart faster doesn’t raise the cardiac output. The heart is needed for cardiac output, but it doesn’t normally limit it.
The veins
  1. Sympathetic venoconstriction raises MSFP two- to three-fold. Click the pressure source Mean systemic filling pressure, and raise it from 7 to 17.5 mmHg (2.5-fold).
  2. The muscle pump about halves RvR. Close the switch Muscle pump, which puts a second path of the same resistance beside RvR.
With MSFP at 17.5 mmHg and RvR halved, what are RAP and ABP now? What does each change do in VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR, and what does the heart still have to do?
RAP is back to about 0 mmHg and ABP to 93 mmHg, with 417 mL/s (25 L/min) returning and being pumped. Raising MSFP 2.5-fold raises the pressure that drives venous return, and halving RvR doubles the flow that the same pressure difference can drive, so together they let venous return rise 2.5 × 2 = 5-fold with RAP still close to zero. The heart’s job is to pump that larger venous return without RAP rising. Less vagal activity and more sympathetic activity raise its rate and its contractility, so it can. In Guyton’s curves, the venous return curve and the cardiac function curve both move up, and they cross at a much higher cardiac output with RAP still close to zero. In the body, ABP rises a little in exercise rather than staying the same, because central command resets the baroreflex to a higher set point.
In this model, what sets the highest cardiac output that can be reached with RAP at zero?

If you have time

A haemorrhage
  1. Put the model back to rest. Open both switches, and set MSFP to 7 mmHg and the heart to 83.3 mL/s. Then lose a tenth of the blood. Losing a tenth of the blood volume roughly halves MSFP, because only about the last fifth of the blood volume stretches the walls of the vessels. So set MSFP to 3.5 mmHg.
With the heart still pumping 83.3 mL/s, what is RAP? Now find the heart’s output that brings RAP back to zero. What is it in litres a minute, and what is ABP then?
With the heart at 83.3 mL/s, RAP falls to −3.5 mmHg, which real veins wouldn’t allow. RAP is back at zero when the heart pumps 41.7 mL/s, which is 2.5 L/min, half its resting output, because VR = MSFP / RvR = 3.5 / 0.084. ABP = CO × TPR then falls to about 46.5 mmHg, half its resting value. So a haemorrhage lowers the blood pressure through MSFP, venous return and CO, not through TPR. The baroreflex then acts on each term. Sympathetic venoconstriction raises MSFP again, arteriolar constriction raises TPR, and the heart’s rate and contractility rise.

Write your essay, and hand it in

≈ 50 min

This is the work to hand in this week. As before, you write it in Supervision work, as you will in the online exam, and then hand it in from the same page. First, though, a few minutes on how to use equations in an essay, because this title needs them.

“Describe the cardiovascular responses during sustained exercise. What limits maximal exercise performance under these conditions?”

Thinking about the title

The title has two parts. The first asks you to describe the responses, but the best essays explain them too: what each response does, and why the body needs it. The second asks what limits maximal performance, and that needs an argument. Weigh up the evidence for each possible limit (the lungs, the heart, the muscles and the circulation as a whole), and come to a conclusion. Leave yourself time for it, because it’s where many essays run out of steam.

Sustained exercise here means dynamic exercise with large groups of muscles, kept up for minutes at a time, like the cycling in chapter 1. These questions might help you plan.

  • In the muscles. How does their blood flow rise so fast at the start, and what keeps it up once exercise is sustained?
  • In the whole circulation. What does that do to TPR, and so to ABP? What must the cardiac output do, and how does venous return rise to match it? Chapter 2 has numbers for this.
  • The control. What drives the changes in sympathetic and vagal activity, and why couldn’t feedback from the baroreceptors do it alone? What happens to ABP?
  • The limits. In a fit person, which limits maximal exercise: the lungs, the heart, the muscles or the circulation? What’s the evidence? Is the answer the same for someone who is unfit, or ill?

Using equations in your essay

This title needs equations, and Dr Fraser’s handout suggests building your answer on two of them: ABP = CO × TPR, and CO = VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR. An equation earns its place in an essay when it does some work: when it organises your answer, makes a prediction, or puts a number on something. Written once at the start and never used again, it earns nothing. Here’s how to make one work.

  1. Write it out, and say what each term is. “Venous return is VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR, where MSFP is the mean systemic filling pressure, RAP the right atrial pressure and RvR the resistance to venous return.” Give typical values where you can, such as an MSFP of about 7 mmHg and an RAP close to zero.
  2. Say what it means, and what it doesn’t. ABP = CO × TPR is flow = pressure difference / resistance, applied to the whole systemic circulation, with RAP taken as zero. It says how the three are related, not what causes what. CO = HR × SV is true of every heartbeat, but it doesn’t mean that a faster heart pumps more blood. Pace a heart faster and SV falls.
  3. Use it as a plan. Take the terms one at a time. For each one, ask what happens to it in exercise, by what mechanism, and by how much. Then the equation tells you what has to happen to the others.
  4. Put numbers in. If TPR falls to a fifth of its resting value and ABP is to stay up, the cardiac output has to rise five-fold. If MSFP rises 2.5-fold and RvR halves, venous return can rise 2.5 × 2 = 5-fold. Say where your numbers come from.
  5. Use it to argue. An equation shows what can happen and what can’t. Venous return can’t rise just because the heart pumps harder, because RAP can’t fall much below zero. That’s the kind of point the second half of the title asks for.
One equation, quoted and used

Quoted: “Haemorrhage lowers the blood pressure, as ABP = CO × TPR. The baroreceptors detect the fall and the sympathetic nervous system is activated, which increases heart rate, contractility and TPR, so the blood pressure returns to normal.”

Used: “A haemorrhage lowers the arterial pressure through the cardiac output, not through TPR. Venous return is VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR, and MSFP depends on the stressed volume, only about the last fifth of the blood volume, so losing a tenth of the blood roughly halves MSFP. RAP is already close to zero, so before any reflex acts, venous return falls by about half, and so do the cardiac output and, by ABP = CO × TPR, the arterial pressure. The baroreflex then acts on each term. Sympathetic venoconstriction raises MSFP again, arteriolar constriction raises TPR, and a faster, more forceful heart pumps the restored venous return without RAP rising.”

The first quotes an equation and moves on. The second says which term changes and why, puts a number on it, and shows how each response acts on a term of the equations. Your essay can do the same for exercise.

Here are three slips that essays on this topic often make. What’s wrong with each?

“In exercise the cardiac output rises because the heart beats faster (CO = HR × SV).”
“Sympathetic venoconstriction raises the blood pressure by increasing TPR.”
“In exercise the muscle pump raises MSFP.”

Once you’ve read the guidance, where in your essay could an equation have done more work, and what would it have shown?
There’s no single right answer here, so bring yours to the supervision. Many essays on this title quote ABP = CO × TPR at the start, then describe the responses without coming back to it. The equations can carry the whole first half: TPR falls to about a fifth, so the cardiac output has to rise about five-fold, and VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR shows how (MSFP up 2.5-fold, RvR halved, RAP held near zero by the heart). In the second half, the same equation shows why the heart alone can’t raise the cardiac output, and O2 uptake = flow × the O2 extracted from each litre of blood shows why the one-leg and two-leg experiments point to the circulation as the limit.

Short-answer questions

These three go with this week’s lectures, one with each. Each is marked out of five, so answer in bullet points and aim for at least five separate points. Each one can be answered with an equation as its frame.
