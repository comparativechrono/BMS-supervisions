Why the clock?

Your body is not the same at four in the morning as at four in the afternoon. Your core temperature, blood pressure and heart rate, and the levels of cortisol and melatonin in your blood, rise and fall through every day, driven by a master clock in your hypothalamus: the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). The clock is built from negative feedback; it is set each day by light; and it acts through the autonomic nervous system, the pituitary and hormones. So it touches every one of this week’s lectures – and this week we look at them through the clock.

A clock made of negative feedback

≈ 15 min

Negative feedback holds things steady – unless it is slow. Add a delay and it overshoots; add a long one and it never settles. Run two loops from the lectures: thirst, and the clock in your cells.

Thirst: a slow loop, and feed-forward

In the first lecture you met a problem with drinking. When you sweat, the osmolarity of your extracellular fluid (ECF) rises; osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect it, and you feel thirsty and drink. But water takes time to be absorbed from the gut – and until it has been, the osmoreceptors go on telling you that you need it.

Run the thirst loop
  1. Open the diagram . The comparator (Σ) compares the osmolarity of the ECF with its set point, 300 mOsm/L, and the difference makes you thirsty.
  2. Press Run , and watch ECF osmolarity in the graphs under the diagram. You can drag the slider under the graphs to see the diagram at any moment.
  3. The grey arrow labelled feed-forward, from the mouth and stomach receptors, is blocked. Right-click it (or press and hold it), choose Unblock this arrow, and run the diagram again.
With negative feedback alone, what happened to the osmolarity once you started to drink, and why? What did the feed-forward arrow change?
With negative feedback alone, the osmolarity overshot: it fell to about 291 mOsm/L, about as far below the set point as sweating had raised it above it (to about 309), before it came back to 300. The water takes about 15 minutes to be absorbed, and all that time the osmoreceptors still report a high osmolarity, so you go on drinking: by the time the water reaches the blood, you have drunk too much. Negative feedback with a delay overshoots, and can oscillate. With feed-forward, receptors in the mouth and stomach signal that water has been drunk before it has been absorbed, and switch thirst off for a while: you drink less, and the overshoot almost disappears (the lowest point is about 298 mOsm/L). This is what Bellows (1938) found in dogs with oesophageal fistulae, whose water never reached the stomach: they drank about the right amount and then stopped – but only for a while.

A clock in every cell

Almost every cell in your body has a clock, and it is built from a negative feedback loop of genes and proteins (a transcription–translation feedback loop). Two proteins, CLOCK and BMAL1, switch on the Per and Cry genes. Their mRNA is made into the PER and CRY proteins, which – hours later – go back into the nucleus and switch their own genes off. When the proteins have been broken down, the genes come on again, and the cycle starts again.

Why a slow loop makes a clock

Without delays, negative feedback settles to a steady level. But each step of this loop takes hours: transcription, translation, the changes made to the proteins, and their journey back into the nucleus. By the time the proteins switch the genes off there are far too many of them, and by the time they have gone, the genes have been off for too long. So the loop overshoots one way and then the other, for ever: it oscillates, with a period set mainly by its delays – about a day.

Run the clock
  1. Open the clock . (If it asks whether to leave the thirst diagram, you needn’t save it.) The arrow from Light is blocked: the clock is in a cave, with no daylight to set it.
  2. Run it . The message under the diagram gives the period of the oscillation, from one peak to the next.
What is the period of the clock in the cave, in hours?
About 24.6 hours. Without daylight, a clock runs at its own pace, a little different from 24 hours – which is why these rhythms are called circadian (from circa diem, about a day). In 1962 the French caver Michel Siffre lived for 63 days in a cave, with no daylight and no clock. He went on sleeping and waking in a regular cycle, but it was a little longer than 24 hours, so his days slipped later and later: when he came out, he thought he had been underground for much less time than he had.

The first clock gene, period, was found in fruit flies by Ronald Konopka and Seymour Benzer in 1971. One mutant fly had a rhythm of about 19 hours (perS), another of about 29 hours (perL), and a third had no rhythm at all (per0). Which change to the model made a clock like per0?

Out of the cave, what was the period? What does light do to the clock, and why does the clock need it?
24 hours. Light resets – entrains – the clock each day, so that it keeps to 24 hours and stays in step with the Sun: light is the clock’s main zeitgeber (“time-giver”). A clock with a period of 24.6 hours that was never reset would drift by about 36 minutes a day, and within a few weeks it would be telling you that midnight was noon. In mammals the light reaches the master clock, the SCN, through the eyes (you will follow its path in the next chapter). Many people who are totally blind have clocks that free-run, drifting in and out of step with the day.
A Nobel Prize for the loop

In 2017 Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering how the clock works: the PER protein builds up at night, enters the nucleus and switches off its own gene – the loop you have just run. Clocks are found in animals, plants, fungi and some bacteria: their genes differ, but most are built round a delayed negative feedback loop like this one.

For your essay

A clock is negative feedback that is meant to oscillate: a long delay turns a loop that would hold things steady into one that keeps time. Thirst is negative feedback that would overshoot if feed-forward didn’t help it. Both say something about the strengths and the limits of negative feedback.

Once clotting starts, what happens to it, and why can’t a loop like this hold anything steady?
It runs away: each step makes more of what activates it, so the response grows faster and faster until something outside the loop stops it – in the body, anticoagulants, which keep the clot to the wound. Positive feedback reinforces a change instead of opposing it, so it can’t return a variable to a set point – it is useful where a fast, all-or-nothing change is needed, and it is always brought to an end.

Where is the clock?

≈ 20 min

Your master clock is a pair of tiny nuclei in the hypothalamus. Find where they are, follow the light that sets them, and visit the glands they drive – in 3D, under the microscope and as molecules.

The master clock

The suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN) are two small clusters of neurons, one on each side of the third ventricle, at the bottom of the hypothalamus. They sit just above the optic chiasm, where the optic nerves meet and cross – hence their name (supra, above). They are too small to be in the model, but you can find exactly where they are.

Light sets the clock. Follow its path: from which cells of the eye, by which route, to where?
Some of the ganglion cells of the retina contain a photopigment of their own, melanopsin, which responds best to blue light. These intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) send their axons along the optic nerve to the optic chiasm, where they leave it in the retinohypothalamic tract for the SCN just above. The SCN’s neurons fire faster by day than at night, and they pass the time of day on to the rest of the body – to the pineal gland, for example, through the paraventricular nucleus, the spinal cord and sympathetic nerves, which is why melatonin is made at night.

Why does the posterior pituitary look so different from the anterior pituitary?
The posterior pituitary is nervous tissue, an extension of the hypothalamus: it is made of the axons and nerve terminals of neurons whose cell bodies lie in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, with glial cells (pituicytes) among them – so it looks pale and fibrous, with few nuclei. Its nerve terminals release ADH and oxytocin into the blood. The anterior pituitary is a gland: clusters and cords of endocrine cells, with many nuclei and stained cytoplasm, between wide capillaries (sinusoids) that bring the releasing hormones of the hypothalamus down the portal vessels.
Cortisol rises in the early morning, before you wake, preparing you for the day. Which part of the adrenal gland makes it?

Two hormones of the day and the night

Melatonin and cortisol
  1. Open melatonin , which the pineal gland makes from tryptophan, by way of serotonin. Its level in the blood rises at night, to about 80 pg/mL.
  2. Now open cortisol , a steroid made from cholesterol.
Cortisol is a steroid, so it is lipid-soluble. Which of these follows from that?
Melatonin, an indoleamine, is lipid-soluble too, so it isn’t stored either: it leaves the pineal gland as soon as it is made, and its level in the blood follows how fast it is being made – high at night, low by day. (It acts mainly on receptors in the membranes of its target cells, including those of the SCN: melatonin feeds back on the clock.)

Why might using a bright screen late in the evening make it harder to fall asleep?
Screens give out a lot of blue light, which melanopsin responds to best. Light in the evening reaches the SCN through the retinohypothalamic tract: it suppresses the release of melatonin and shifts the clock later, so you feel sleepy later. Experiments in which people read from a light-emitting screen for a few hours before bed have found just this – less melatonin, and a later clock – though how much a screen matters depends on how bright it is and how long you look at it.

Your heart’s daily rhythm

≈ 20 min

Your heart beats more slowly at night. With real 24-hour ECGs from 18 people, see the rhythm for yourself – in a few lines of R that are typed for you.

From beats to a heart rate

The data come from the MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm Database: long-term ECGs of 18 adults, each recorded for about a day as they went about their lives, with every beat labelled. A heart rate comes from the time between beats, the R–R interval: in beats per minute, it is 60 divided by the R–R interval in seconds. Measure one yourself first.

Measure an R–R interval
  1. Open an ECG : four seconds of a normal heartbeat.
  2. Choose the Calipers and drag from the peak of one R wave (the tall spike) to the peak of the next. The bar above the trace shows the interval (Δt) and the heart rate it gives.
What heart rate does an R–R interval of 0.8 s give, in beats per minute?
75 beats per minute: 60 ÷ 0.8 = 75. In the data set, the heart rate of each hour is 60 ÷ the mean of the R–R intervals in that hour – thousands of them (the column n_beats says how many).

Describe her day. When was her heart rate lowest, and when was it highest?
It was low and steady through the night (61–65 beats per minute, lowest at 05:00), jumped at 08:00 (from 63 to 77) – about when she got up – stayed higher through the day, highest in the afternoon (88 at 15:00), and fell through the evening, to 65 by 23:00. (The hour from 11:00 is missing.)
Everyone’s average day
  1. Work out the mean of the 18 people for each hour: avg <- aggregate(hr ~ hour, data = heart, FUN = mean) . Then look at it: avg . aggregate() splits the heart rates up by hour and takes the mean of each group, leaving out the NAs.
  2. Plot it: plot(avg$hour, avg$hr, type = "b", xlab = "Hour of the day", ylab = "Mean heart rate (beats/min)")
  3. Which hour has the lowest mean? avg$hour[which.min(avg$hr)]
Which hour of the day has the lowest mean heart rate (0 to 23)?
5, that is 05:00 to 06:00, at 64.4 beats per minute – although 03:00 and 04:00 are almost as low (64.6): the trough is in the small hours, when most people are asleep. The mean is highest in the late morning and early afternoon, at about 85 beats per minute.

  1. The mean heart rate by day, from 08:00 to 20:00: day <- mean(heart$hr[heart$hour >= 8 & heart$hour < 20], na.rm = TRUE)
  2. … and at night, from midnight to 06:00: night <- mean(heart$hr[heart$hour < 6], na.rm = TRUE)
  3. The difference: day - night . (na.rm = TRUE tells mean() to leave out the NAs: the mean of anything that includes an NA is NA.)
How much lower is the heart rate at night than by day, in beats per minute?
About 17 beats per minute: 82.5 by day against 65.6 at night, so the heart rate is about a fifth lower at night. Every one of the 18 people was lower at night, by between 7 and 31 beats per minute.

If you have time

How people differ, and a curve for the rhythm
  1. How much do people differ? boxplot(hr ~ hour, data = heart, xlab = "Hour of the day", ylab = "Heart rate (beats/min)") : a box for each hour, showing the middle half of the 18 people (the box), the median (the line) and the range (the whiskers).
  2. Circadian biologists describe a rhythm by fitting a cosine curve to it (a cosinor). Fit one: fit <- lm(hr ~ cos(2 * pi * hour / 24) + sin(2 * pi * hour / 24), data = heart) . Keep its coefficients: b <- unname(coef(fit)) . Then work out the curve’s mean level (its mesor), its amplitude and the hour of its peak: c(mesor = b[1], amplitude = sqrt(b[2]^2 + b[3]^2), peak = (atan2(b[3], b[2]) * 12 / pi) %% 24)
  3. Plot the means again (plot(avg$hour, avg$hr, type = "b", xlab = "Hour of the day", ylab = "Mean heart rate (beats/min)") ) and draw the curve over them: curve(b[1] + b[2] * cos(2 * pi * x / 24) + b[3] * sin(2 * pi * x / 24), add = TRUE, col = "red")
What are the amplitude of the rhythm, and the time of its peak? How well does a cosine curve describe it?
The mesor is 75.3 beats per minute, the amplitude 10.1 beats per minute (the curve swings 10 above and 10 below the mesor), and the peak – the acrophase – is at 14.7 hours, about 14:40. A cosine is only a rough fit: the real rhythm isn’t symmetrical. It jumps up in the morning, when people get up, and falls more slowly through the evening.

How essays are marked

≈ 15 min

Your supervisor gives feedback on essays against six core criteria. See what each one means, and how it plays out in an example essay – with the comments it would get, and how to put them right.

When your supervisor gives you feedback on an essay, it is against six core criteria. Each describes a quality of a good essay, and what makes a better one – and they are the qualities examiners look for too.

1Addressing the question

How relevant the content of the essay is to the question or title set.

Good: It selects relevant material: knowledge, concepts, interpretations, models and others’ views.

Better: It makes clear, throughout, how the material is relevant to the question.

2Using evidence

The use of material from outside sources: research findings, facts, examples, quotations.

Good: It includes information from sources that backs up its points.

Better: It interprets the evidence, and shows explicitly how it supports a more general claim.

3Developing argument

Building a coherent, convincing case for a point of view: an analytical path from a starting point to a conclusion.

Good: It takes a position on the issues the title raises.

Better: It develops its argument throughout, each part building on the last.

4Critical evaluation

Judging the value, significance, strengths and weaknesses of something: a finding, a theory, a method, an interpretation.

Good: It makes evaluative points about the strengths and weaknesses of what it discusses.

Better: It explains and justifies its evaluations, systematically.

5Structuring

The arrangement of the essay into paragraphs.

Good: It has a clear introduction and conclusion, and each paragraph of the main body has one main point.

Better: The order of its paragraphs follows the development of the argument.

6Use of language

Words, grammar and punctuation suited to the purpose.

Good: It is free of errors of spelling, punctuation and grammar.

Better: It uses an academic style, in the register of the subject.

The core criteria and the comments below are from the essay feedback form of Assessment Plus: using assessment criteria to support student learning, a HEFCE-funded consortium project (assessmentplus.net).

An example essay

Here is a short essay on a question from this week’s lectures. Its facts are right – but it has a problem under each of the six criteria. Read it, and then decide which comment from the feedback form fits each part best.

Why do animals have circadian rhythms?

1Circadian rhythms are really interesting and are found in loads of animals, as well as in plants and even some bacteria. A circadian rhythm is a rhythm with a period of about 24 hours. In this essay I will talk about circadian rhythms.

2Circadian rhythms are made by a feedback loop of genes and proteins. In mammals, CLOCK and BMAL1 switch on the Per and Cry genes, and the PER and CRY proteins go back into the nucleus and switch their own genes off. The master clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus, which gets information about light from retinal ganglion cells that contain melanopsin.

3Michel Siffre lived in a cave for 63 days in 1962. Shift workers have more health problems than other people. Blood pressure is higher in the morning, and heart attacks are more common then.

4Circadian rhythms obviously evolved because they help animals to survive. For example, cortisol goes up in the morning to get us ready to wake up, and melatonin goes up at night.

5In conclusion, circadian rhythms are found in lots of animals and there are lots of reasons why they have them, so they are very important.

What comments would it get?

Addressing the question. Paragraph 2 is accurate. Which comment fits it best?
To put it right, make the mechanism answer the question: “Because the rhythm is generated within the body – by a negative feedback loop of genes and proteins that takes about a day to go round – the body can prepare for dawn before it comes, rather than respond once the light has arrived.”
Using evidence. Paragraph 3 mentions Siffre’s cave, shift workers and the morning rise in blood pressure. Which comment fits it best?
To put it right, say what each piece of evidence shows: “The rhythms are generated within the body: in 1962 Michel Siffre spent 63 days in a cave, without daylight or clocks, and he went on sleeping and waking in a regular cycle – but one a little longer than 24 hours, so it cannot have been driven by the day outside.” Then either support the claim about shift workers, with a source, or leave it out.
Developing argument. Read the conclusion, paragraph 5, and think about the essay as a whole. Which comment fits best?
To put it right, decide on your answer before you write, and state it early: “Animals have circadian rhythms chiefly because they let the body anticipate the predictable changes of each day.” Then make each paragraph a step towards it – the rhythm is made within the body; how it prepares the body; how good the evidence is that this helps – and end with a conclusion that draws those steps together.
Critical evaluation. Paragraph 4 says that circadian rhythms “obviously evolved because they help animals to survive”. Which comment fits best?
To put it right, weigh the evidence: “When strains of cyanobacteria with clocks of different periods were grown together, the strain whose period matched the light–dark cycle outgrew the others (Ouyang et al., 1998); and chipmunks returned to the wild after damage to the SCN were killed by predators more often than intact animals (DeCoursey et al., 2000). Such studies are few, however, and come from only a handful of species.”
Structuring. Paragraph 1 is the introduction. Which comment fits it best?
To put it right, define the terms and say where the essay is going: “Circadian rhythms are rhythms with a period of about 24 hours that persist without cues from the environment. This essay argues that animals have them chiefly because they allow the body to anticipate the day: it shows first that the rhythms are generated within the body, then how they prepare it for the day, and finally weighs the evidence that they help animals to survive.”
Use of language. Look at the wording of paragraphs 1 and 4: “really interesting”, “loads of”, “get us ready”. Which comment fits best?
To put it right, use the plain, precise language of science: “Circadian rhythms are found throughout the animal kingdom, as well as in plants, fungi and some bacteria”; “plasma cortisol rises in the early morning, before waking, preparing the body for activity.”

Plagiarism, and why you can’t paste

≈ 10 min

Using someone else’s words, ideas or structure without saying so is plagiarism – even when you have changed the words. Try the University’s exercise: which of four paraphrases of the same passage is acceptable?

The University counts as plagiarism anything you submit as your own that comes, in part or in whole, from the work of others without due acknowledgement – whether or not you meant to deceive. Copying a sentence is the obvious case. Paraphrasing too closely is the one that catches people out.

The original

(1994) Lords of the Fly: Drosophila Genetics and the Experimental Life. The University of Chicago Press.

Why Supervision work won’t let you paste

No pasting from anywhere else

In the online exam you can’t paste in text copied from outside the exam, and Supervision work is the same: text copied from a website, your notes, an AI tool or these instructions won’t go into your essay. (You can still copy and paste within your own essay, to move a sentence or a paragraph.)

So every word of the essay you hand in is one you typed – which is the best protection against plagiarising by accident. When you use someone’s finding or idea, put it in your own words and say whose it is: “Konopka and Benzer (1971) found…”. You will see it for yourself when you start your essay.

Write your essay, and hand it in

≈ 45 min

The one piece of work to hand in this week. Write it in Supervision work, as you will write in the online exam; then hand it in from the same page: you upload it to Tim Hearn’s Dropbox.

“Discuss, with examples, why negative feedback mechanisms are more common than positive feedback mechanisms in animal physiology.”

Why write it like this?

Your exams are written on a computer, in an online exam system: you type your answers in one sitting, against the clock, without notes, and you can’t paste anything in. Supervision work is set up in the same way. Writing your supervision essays like this makes the exam familiar long before the summer: you find out how much you can plan and write in 40 minutes, and what it is like to do it without your notes. And because your supervisor sees the essay you can write in that time, the feedback is about the essay you would actually write in the exam – not one polished over a week. As in the exam, you submit it when you have finished, and it is handed in from the same page: here, you upload it to Tim Hearn’s Dropbox.

Before you start

  • Read how essays are marked , and about plagiarism .
  • Revise the lectures first if you like. Then put your notes away, and find about 45 minutes when you won’t be interrupted.
  • Have the hand-in password that Tim Hearn gave you ready, for handing it in at the end. It isn’t on this site.
  • There is no time limit, but give yourself about 40 minutes, as in the exam. The clock at the top of the paper counts the time you spend writing, and tells you when you reach 40 minutes. If you haven’t finished by then, write your conclusion – or carry on if you need to: the clock shows how long you took.
  • Spend the first five minutes planning. What is your answer to the question? Which examples support it, and in what order will you make your points? You can plan on paper, or in the box itself, and delete the plan before you submit.

Short-answer questions

A short-answer question (SAQ) asks for a focused answer in a few minutes: the definitions, the key points and an example or two, with no introduction or conclusion. These five go with this week’s lectures:

  1. Define ‘feedforward’ and ‘negative feedback’, and explain how these processes relate to thirst.
  2. What are the ways in which solutes can cross cell membranes, and how do they differ?
  3. What are the theoretical problems that you might encounter in designing and interpreting an experiment to investigate the effects of a hormone on the body?
  4. How is the pituitary gland controlled?
  5. What is Addison’s disease? What is Conn’s syndrome?

They are a paper of their own in Supervision work , with no time limit: about 10 minutes for each is a good aim, and you can answer as many as you like. After you submit, Review my answers shows what a good answer covers.

Essay titles for revision
