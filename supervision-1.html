Why the clock?

Your body isn’t the same at four in the morning as it is at four in the afternoon. Your core temperature, blood pressure and heart rate rise and fall over the course of every day, and so do the levels of cortisol and melatonin in your blood. All of this is driven by a master clock in your hypothalamus, the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). The clock itself is built from negative feedback. It’s reset every day by light, and it works through the autonomic nervous system, the pituitary and hormones. That means it touches every one of this week’s lectures, which is why we’re using it to tie them together.

A clock made of negative feedback

≈ 15 min

Negative feedback keeps things steady, as long as it’s quick. Add a delay and it overshoots, and with a long enough delay it never settles at all. In this chapter you’ll run two loops from the lectures: thirst, and the clock inside your cells.

Thirst: a slow loop, and feed-forward

In the first lecture you met a problem with drinking. When you sweat, the osmolarity of your extracellular fluid (ECF) rises. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect the change, so you feel thirsty and drink. But water takes time to be absorbed from the gut, and until it has been, the osmoreceptors keep telling you that you need more.

Run the thirst loop
  1. Open the diagram . The comparator (Σ) compares the osmolarity of the ECF with its set point of 300 mOsm/L, and the difference between them is what makes you thirsty.
  2. Press Run and watch ECF osmolarity in the graphs below the diagram. Dragging the slider under the graphs shows you the diagram at any moment of the run.
  3. The grey arrow labelled feed-forward, which comes from receptors in the mouth and stomach, is blocked at the moment. Right-click it (or press and hold it), choose Unblock this arrow, and run the diagram again.
With negative feedback alone, what happened to the osmolarity once you started to drink, and why? What did the feed-forward arrow change?
With negative feedback alone, the osmolarity overshot. Sweating had raised it to about 309 mOsm/L. Once you started drinking, it fell to about 291, roughly as far below the set point as it had been above it, before settling back at 300. The water takes about 15 minutes to be absorbed, and all that time the osmoreceptors are still reporting a high osmolarity, so you keep drinking. By the time the water reaches your blood, you’ve drunk too much. That’s typical of negative feedback with a delay: it overshoots, and it can oscillate. With feed-forward, receptors in the mouth and stomach signal that you’ve drunk water before it has been absorbed, and they switch thirst off for a while. You drink less, and the overshoot almost disappears (the lowest point is about 298 mOsm/L). Bellows (1938) saw this in dogs with oesophageal fistulae, whose water never reached the stomach. They drank about the right amount and then stopped, but only for a while.

A clock in every cell

Almost every cell in your body has a clock of its own, built from a negative feedback loop of genes and proteins (a transcription–translation feedback loop). Two proteins, CLOCK and BMAL1, switch on the Per and Cry genes. The mRNA from these genes is translated into the PER and CRY proteins, which go back into the nucleus hours later and switch their own genes off. Once those proteins have been broken down, the genes come back on and the cycle begins again.

Why a slow loop makes a clock

Without delays, negative feedback settles at a steady level. Every step of this loop takes hours, though: transcription, translation, the changes made to the proteins, and their journey back into the nucleus. By the time the proteins switch their genes off, far too many of them have been made. And by the time they’ve gone, the genes have been off for too long. So the loop keeps overshooting, first one way and then the other. In other words, it oscillates, with a period of about a day that’s set mainly by its delays.

Run the clock
  1. Open the clock . If it asks whether you want to leave the thirst diagram, choose Go on without saving. The arrow from Light is blocked, so the clock is in a cave, with no daylight to set it.
  2. Run it . The message under the diagram tells you the period of the oscillation, measured from one peak to the next.
What is the period of the clock in the cave, in hours?
About 24.6 hours. Without daylight, a clock runs at its own pace, which is a little different from 24 hours. That’s why these rhythms are called circadian, from circa diem, “about a day”. In 1962 the French caver Michel Siffre spent 63 days in a cave with no daylight and no clock. He kept sleeping and waking in a regular cycle, but it was a little longer than 24 hours, so his days slipped later and later. When he came out, he thought he’d been underground for far less time than he had.

The first clock gene, period, was found in fruit flies by Ronald Konopka and Seymour Benzer in 1971. One mutant fly had a rhythm of about 19 hours (perS), another of about 29 hours (perL), and a third had no rhythm at all (per0). Which change to the model made a clock like per0?

Out of the cave, what was the period? What does light do to the clock, and why does the clock need it?
24 hours. Light resets, or entrains, the clock every day, so it keeps to 24 hours and stays in step with the Sun. That makes light the clock’s main zeitgeber (“time-giver”). A clock with a period of 24.6 hours that was never reset would drift by about 36 minutes a day, and within a few weeks it would be telling you that midnight was noon. In mammals, light reaches the master clock in the SCN through the eyes, and you’ll follow its path in the next chapter. Many people who are totally blind have clocks that free-run, drifting in and out of step with the day.
A Nobel Prize for the loop

In 2017 Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering how the clock works. They showed that the PER protein builds up at night, enters the nucleus and switches off its own gene, which is the loop you’ve just run. Clocks have been found in animals, plants, fungi and some bacteria. Their genes differ, but most are built around a delayed negative feedback loop like this one.

For your essay

A clock is negative feedback that’s meant to oscillate. A long delay is what turns a loop that would hold things steady into one that keeps time. Thirst is negative feedback that would overshoot if feed-forward didn’t help it out. Both examples tell you something about what negative feedback does well, and where it falls short.

Once clotting starts, what happens to it, and why can’t a loop like this hold anything steady?
It runs away. Each step makes more of whatever activates it, so the response grows faster and faster until something outside the loop stops it. In the body, that’s the job of anticoagulants, which keep the clot to the wound. Positive feedback reinforces a change instead of opposing it, so it can’t bring a variable back to a set point. It’s useful when you need a fast, all-or-nothing change, and something always has to bring it to an end.

Where is the clock?

≈ 20 min

Your master clock is a pair of tiny nuclei in the hypothalamus. In this chapter you’ll find where they are, follow the light that sets them, and look at the glands they control, in 3D, under the microscope and as molecules.

The master clock

The suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN) are two small clusters of neurons at the bottom of the hypothalamus, one on each side of the third ventricle. They sit just above the optic chiasm, where the optic nerves meet and cross, and that’s how they got their name (supra means “above”). They’re too small to appear in the model, but you can still find exactly where they are.

Light sets the clock. Can you trace its path? Which cells in the eye pick it up, which route does the signal take, and where does it end up?
Some of the ganglion cells in the retina have a photopigment of their own, melanopsin, which responds best to blue light. These intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) send their axons along the optic nerve to the optic chiasm. There they leave it in the retinohypothalamic tract and go to the SCN just above. The SCN’s neurons fire faster by day than at night, and they pass the time of day on to the rest of the body. The pineal gland, for example, gets the message through the paraventricular nucleus, the spinal cord and sympathetic nerves, which is why melatonin is made at night.

Why does the posterior pituitary look so different from the anterior pituitary?
The posterior pituitary is nervous tissue, an extension of the hypothalamus. It’s made of the axons and nerve terminals of neurons whose cell bodies lie in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, with glial cells (pituicytes) among them, so it looks pale and fibrous, with few nuclei. Its nerve terminals release ADH and oxytocin into the blood. The anterior pituitary, on the other hand, is a gland. It’s made of clusters and cords of endocrine cells, with many nuclei and stained cytoplasm, lying between wide capillaries (sinusoids). The portal vessels bring the releasing hormones of the hypothalamus down to these capillaries.
Cortisol rises in the early morning, before you wake, preparing you for the day. Which part of the adrenal gland makes it?

Two hormones of the day and the night

Melatonin and cortisol
  1. Open melatonin . The pineal gland makes it from tryptophan, by way of serotonin, and its level in the blood rises to about 80 pg/mL at night.
  2. Now open cortisol . It’s a steroid, made from cholesterol.
Cortisol is a steroid, which makes it lipid-soluble. Which of these follows from that?
Melatonin, an indoleamine, is lipid-soluble too, so it isn’t stored either. It leaves the pineal gland as soon as it’s made, which means its level in the blood follows how fast it’s being made: high at night and low by day. It acts mainly on receptors in the membranes of its target cells, including cells in the SCN, so melatonin feeds back on the clock as well.

Why might using a bright screen late in the evening make it harder to fall asleep?
Screens give out a lot of blue light, the colour melanopsin responds to best. Light in the evening reaches the SCN through the retinohypothalamic tract. That suppresses the release of melatonin and shifts the clock later, so you feel sleepy later. In experiments where people read from a light-emitting screen for a few hours before bed, they made less melatonin, and their clocks shifted later. How much a screen matters depends on how bright it is and how long you look at it, though.

Your heart’s daily rhythm

≈ 20 min

Your heart beats more slowly at night. Using real 24-hour ECGs from 18 people, you’ll see this rhythm for yourself in a few lines of R, which the page types for you.

From beats to a heart rate

The data come from the MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm Database, a set of long-term ECGs from 18 adults. Each one was recorded over about a day while the person went about their life, and every beat has been labelled. A heart rate comes from the time between beats, the R–R interval. The heart rate in beats per minute is 60 divided by the R–R interval in seconds. Start by measuring one yourself.

Measure an R–R interval
  1. Open an ECG . It shows four seconds of a normal heartbeat.
  2. Choose the Calipers and drag from the peak of one R wave (the tall spike) to the peak of the next. The bar above the trace shows the interval (Δt) and the heart rate that goes with it.
What heart rate does an R–R interval of 0.8 s give, in beats per minute?
75 beats per minute, because 60 ÷ 0.8 = 75. In the data set, the heart rate for each hour is 60 divided by the mean of all the R–R intervals in that hour. That’s thousands of them, and the n_beats column tells you exactly how many.

A day and a night, in R

The first time

The first time you open RStudio, the workbench has to download R itself (about 30 MB), so it can take a minute.

The file ~/data/heart-rate-24h.csv, which is already in your files, holds the heart rate of each of the 18 people for each hour of the day. That makes 432 rows (18 people × 24 hours). Each show me button types a line of R into RStudio’s Console for you. Press Enter to run it, and look at what comes back.

Read the data, and plot one person’s day
  1. Read the file into a table called heart: heart <- read.csv("~/data/heart-rate-24h.csv")
  2. Look at its first few rows: head(heart) . Each row is one hour for one person. The columns are subject (the person), sex, age, hour (0 means midnight to 1 a.m.), time, hr (the heart rate in beats per minute) and n_beats.
  3. Summarise the heart rates: summary(heart$hr) . Here heart$hr means “the hr column of the table heart”. Some hours are NA (not available), either because the recording had ended or because it was too noisy.
  4. Pick out one person, a woman of 32: one <- subset(heart, subject == 16786) . Then plot her day: plot(one$hour, one$hr, type = "b", xlab = "Hour of the day", ylab = "Heart rate (beats/min)")
Describe her day. When was her heart rate lowest, and when was it highest?
Her heart rate was low and steady through the night, at 61–65 beats per minute, and lowest at 05:00. It jumped from 63 to 77 at 08:00, around the time she got up, and stayed higher through the day, peaking at 88 at 15:00. Then it fell through the evening, down to 65 by 23:00. The hour from 11:00 is missing.
Everyone’s average day
  1. Work out the mean of the 18 people for each hour: avg <- aggregate(hr ~ hour, data = heart, FUN = mean) . Then have a look at it: avg . The aggregate() function splits the heart rates up by hour and takes the mean of each group, leaving out the NAs.
  2. Plot it: plot(avg$hour, avg$hr, type = "b", xlab = "Hour of the day", ylab = "Mean heart rate (beats/min)")
  3. Which hour has the lowest mean? avg$hour[which.min(avg$hr)]
Which hour of the day has the lowest mean heart rate (0 to 23)?
Hour 5, that is from 05:00 to 06:00, at 64.4 beats per minute. The hours starting at 03:00 and 04:00 are almost as low (64.6), so the trough comes in the small hours, when most people are asleep. The mean is highest in the late morning and early afternoon, at about 85 beats per minute.
Day against night
  1. Work out the mean heart rate by day, from 08:00 to 20:00: day <- mean(heart$hr[heart$hour >= 8 & heart$hour < 20], na.rm = TRUE)
  2. Then work out the mean at night, from midnight to 06:00: night <- mean(heart$hr[heart$hour < 6], na.rm = TRUE)
  3. Finally, work out the difference: day - night . (na.rm = TRUE tells mean() to leave out the NAs, because the mean of anything that includes an NA is NA.)
How much lower is the heart rate at night than by day, in beats per minute?
About 17 beats per minute (82.5 by day against 65.6 at night), so the heart rate is about a fifth lower at night. Every one of the 18 people had a lower heart rate at night, by between 7 and 31 beats per minute.
Does this show that the heart rate has a circadian rhythm, one that’s generated by the body’s clock?

If you have time

How people differ, and a curve for the rhythm
  1. How much do people differ? Try boxplot(hr ~ hour, data = heart, xlab = "Hour of the day", ylab = "Heart rate (beats/min)") . You get a box for each hour: the box covers the middle half of the 18 people, the line inside it is the median, and the whiskers reach out to the furthest values within one and a half box-lengths of the box. Anything further out is drawn as a separate point.
  2. Circadian biologists often describe a rhythm by fitting a cosine curve to it, which they call a cosinor. Fit one: fit <- lm(hr ~ cos(2 * pi * hour / 24) + sin(2 * pi * hour / 24), data = heart) . Keep its coefficients: b <- unname(coef(fit)) . Then work out the curve’s mean level (its mesor), its amplitude, and the hour of its peak: c(mesor = b[1], amplitude = sqrt(b[2]^2 + b[3]^2), peak = (atan2(b[3], b[2]) * 12 / pi) %% 24)
  3. Plot the means again (plot(avg$hour, avg$hr, type = "b", xlab = "Hour of the day", ylab = "Mean heart rate (beats/min)") ) and draw the curve over them: curve(b[1] + b[2] * cos(2 * pi * x / 24) + b[3] * sin(2 * pi * x / 24), add = TRUE, col = "red")
What’s the amplitude of the rhythm, and when does it peak? How well does a cosine curve describe it?
The mesor is 75.3 beats per minute and the amplitude is 10.1 beats per minute, so the curve swings about 10 above and 10 below the mesor. The peak, called the acrophase, is at 14.7 hours, which is about 14:40. A cosine is only a rough fit, because the real rhythm isn’t symmetrical. It jumps up in the morning when people get up, then falls more slowly through the evening.

How essays are marked

≈ 15 min

Your supervisor gives feedback on essays against six core criteria. Here you’ll see what each one means, and how it applies to an example essay: the comments the essay would get, and how to put each problem right.

Each of the six core criteria describes a quality of a good essay and what would make it better, and examiners look for the same qualities.

1Addressing the question

How relevant the content of the essay is to the question or title set.

Good: It selects relevant material: knowledge, concepts, interpretations, models and others’ views.

Better: It makes clear, throughout, how the material is relevant to the question.

2Using evidence

The use of material from outside sources: research findings, facts, examples, quotations.

Good: It includes information from sources that backs up its points.

Better: It interprets the evidence, and shows explicitly how it supports a more general claim.

3Developing argument

Building a coherent, convincing case for a point of view: an analytical path from a starting point to a conclusion.

Good: It takes a position on the issues the title raises.

Better: It develops its argument throughout, each part building on the last.

4Critical evaluation

Judging the value, significance, strengths and weaknesses of something: a finding, a theory, a method, an interpretation.

Good: It makes evaluative points about the strengths and weaknesses of what it discusses.

Better: It explains and justifies its evaluations, systematically.

5Structuring

The arrangement of the essay into paragraphs.

Good: It has a clear introduction and conclusion, and each paragraph of the main body has one main point.

Better: The order of its paragraphs follows the development of the argument.

6Use of language

Words, grammar and punctuation suited to the purpose.

Good: It is free of errors of spelling, punctuation and grammar.

Better: It uses an academic style, in the register of the subject.

The core criteria, and the comments below, come from the essay feedback form of Assessment Plus: using assessment criteria to support student learning, a HEFCE-funded consortium project (assessmentplus.net).

An example essay

Below is a short essay on a question from this week’s lectures. Its facts are all correct, but it has a problem under each of the six criteria. Read it through, then decide which comment from the feedback form fits each part best.

Why do animals have circadian rhythms?

1Circadian rhythms are really interesting and are found in loads of animals, as well as in plants and even some bacteria. A circadian rhythm is a rhythm with a period of about 24 hours. In this essay I will talk about circadian rhythms.

2Circadian rhythms are made by a feedback loop of genes and proteins. In mammals, CLOCK and BMAL1 switch on the Per and Cry genes, and the PER and CRY proteins go back into the nucleus and switch their own genes off. The master clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus, which gets information about light from retinal ganglion cells that contain melanopsin.

3Michel Siffre lived in a cave for 63 days in 1962. Shift workers have more health problems than other people. Blood pressure is higher in the morning, and heart attacks are more common then.

4Circadian rhythms obviously evolved because they help animals to survive. For example, cortisol goes up in the morning to get us ready to wake up, and melatonin goes up at night.

5In conclusion, circadian rhythms are found in lots of animals and there are lots of reasons why they have them, so they are very important.

What comments would it get?

Addressing the question. Paragraph 2 is accurate, but which comment fits it best?
To put it right, use the mechanism to answer the question, perhaps like this: “Because the rhythm is generated within the body – by a negative feedback loop of genes and proteins that takes about a day to go round – the body can prepare for dawn before it comes, rather than respond once the light has arrived.”
Using evidence. Paragraph 3 mentions Siffre’s cave, shift workers and the morning rise in blood pressure. Which comment fits it best?
To put it right, say what each piece of evidence shows. For example: “The rhythms are generated within the body: in 1962 Michel Siffre spent 63 days in a cave, without daylight or clocks, and he went on sleeping and waking in a regular cycle – but one a little longer than 24 hours, so it cannot have been driven by the day outside.” Then either back up the claim about shift workers with a source, or leave it out.
Developing argument. Read the conclusion (paragraph 5) and think about the essay as a whole. Which comment fits best?
To put it right, decide on your answer before you start writing, and say what it is early on. You might write: “Animals have circadian rhythms chiefly because they let the body anticipate the predictable changes of each day.” Then make each paragraph a step towards that answer. The first might show that the rhythm is made within the body, the next how it prepares the body for the day, and the last how good the evidence is that this helps. Finish with a conclusion that draws those steps together.
Critical evaluation. Paragraph 4 says that circadian rhythms “obviously evolved because they help animals to survive”. Which comment fits best?
To put it right, weigh up the evidence, as in this passage: “When strains of cyanobacteria with clocks of different periods were grown together, the strain whose period matched the light–dark cycle outgrew the others (Ouyang et al., 1998); and chipmunks returned to the wild after damage to the SCN were killed by predators more often than intact animals (DeCoursey et al., 2000). Such studies are few, however, and come from only a handful of species.”
Structuring. Paragraph 1 is the introduction. Which comment fits it best?
To put it right, define the terms and say where the essay is going. For example: “Circadian rhythms are rhythms with a period of about 24 hours that persist without cues from the environment. This essay argues that animals have them chiefly because they allow the body to anticipate the day: it shows first that the rhythms are generated within the body, then how they prepare it for the day, and finally weighs the evidence that they help animals to survive.”
Use of language. Look at the wording of paragraphs 1 and 4: “really interesting”, “loads of”, “get us ready”. Which comment fits best?
To put it right, use the plain, precise language of science, such as “Circadian rhythms are found throughout the animal kingdom, as well as in plants, fungi and some bacteria” and “plasma cortisol rises in the early morning, before waking, preparing the body for activity.”

Plagiarism, and why you can’t paste

≈ 10 min

Using someone else’s words, ideas or structure without saying so is plagiarism, even if you’ve changed the words. Try the University’s exercise, and decide which of four paraphrases of the same passage is acceptable.

The University counts as plagiarism anything you submit as your own that comes, in whole or in part, from other people’s work without due acknowledgement, whether or not you meant to deceive anyone. Copying a sentence is the obvious case. Paraphrasing too closely is the one that tends to catch people out.

Why Supervision work won’t let you paste

No pasting from anywhere else

In the online exam you can’t paste in text that you copied from outside the exam, and Supervision work is just the same. Text copied from a website, your notes, an AI tool or these instructions won’t go into your essay, as you’ll see when you start writing. You can still copy and paste within your own essay, to move a sentence or a paragraph around.

That way every word of the essay you hand in is one you typed yourself, which is the best protection against plagiarising by accident. When you use someone’s finding or idea, put it in your own words and say whose it is, as in “Konopka and Benzer (1971) found…”.

Short-answer questions

A short-answer question (SAQ) asks for a focused answer that takes a few minutes to write. You give the definitions, the key points and an example or two, without an introduction or a conclusion. These five go with this week’s lectures:

  1. Define ‘feedforward’ and ‘negative feedback’, and explain how these processes relate to thirst.
  2. What are the ways in which solutes can cross cell membranes, and how do they differ?
  3. What are the theoretical problems that you might encounter in designing and interpreting an experiment to investigate the effects of a hormone on the body?
  4. How is the pituitary gland controlled?
  5. What is Addison’s disease? What is Conn’s syndrome?

They have a paper of their own in Supervision work . There’s no time limit, but about 10 minutes for each is a good aim, and you can answer as many as you like. After you submit, Review my answers shows you what a good answer covers.
